# Chapter 7: Mergers & Acquisitions

## Overview

Mergers and acquisitions are the most consequential financial transactions a company can undertake. They reshape industries, create or destroy enormous shareholder value, and generate most of the high-stakes analytical work done in investment banking and private equity. Yet at their core, M&A transactions reduce to a deceptively simple accounting question: when you pay more for a business than the book value of its net assets, where does the excess go?

Chapter 6 introduced consolidation and goodwill in the context of minority interest. This chapter builds on that foundation to develop a complete framework for M&A accounting and analysis — from the mechanics of the purchase price allocation through to the practical implications for financial modelling, valuation, and due diligence. We also examine why deals are done, why so many destroy value, and what the accounting record of a transaction reveals about the deal thesis.

---

## 1. Why Companies Acquire: The Deal Rationale

Before examining the accounting, it is worth anchoring the mechanics in economic purpose. Acquisitions are justified by one or more of the following rationales:

**Strategic synergies** — the combined entity is worth more than the sum of its parts. Revenue synergies arise when the combined business can cross-sell products, enter new markets, or extract pricing power that neither firm had alone. Cost synergies arise from eliminating duplicated overheads, consolidating procurement, or achieving economies of scale in manufacturing.

**Market consolidation** — acquiring a competitor reduces industry capacity, rationalizes pricing, and improves margins for the surviving players. Horizontal M&A (same industry, same level of the value chain) is often driven by this logic.

**Vertical integration** — acquiring a supplier or customer to control more of the value chain, secure supply, or capture margin that was previously paid to a third party.

**Financial engineering** — a private equity buyer acquires a business using substantial debt (a leveraged buyout), using the target's own cash flows to service that debt while improving operations and selling at a higher multiple. The value creation is a combination of debt paydown, EBITDA growth, and multiple expansion.

**Capability acquisition** — buying access to technology, talent, IP, or a customer base that would take too long to build organically. Common in technology M&A, where paying a large premium for a startup is justified by the time value of not waiting.

The acquisition premium — the amount paid above the target's standalone market value — represents the buyer's bet that their value creation hypothesis will materialize. If it does not, the premium shows up as goodwill impairment. Understanding the deal thesis is therefore the first step in assessing the reasonableness of the accounting that follows.

[[CORE: Acquisition premium = purchase price minus the target's pre-acquisition standalone market value. Represents the buyer's estimate of synergies and strategic value.]]

## 2. The M&A Process: From Negotiation to Consolidation

When BuyerCo acquires a controlling stake (>50%) in TargetCo, the following accounting sequence unfolds on the day of acquisition:

**Step 1 — Determine the purchase price.** This is the total consideration paid: cash, shares issued, deferred payments, earnouts, and any assumed debt. The total is the starting point for everything that follows.

**Step 2 — Identify and revalue TargetCo's identifiable assets and liabilities.** Under IFRS 3 (Business Combinations), all of TargetCo's assets and liabilities must be recognized and measured at **fair value** on the acquisition date. This is the **Purchase Price Allocation (PPA)**. It is one of the most technically complex aspects of M&A accounting:

- **Tangible assets** (PPE, inventory, receivables): revalued from book to fair market value
- **Identifiable intangible assets** that were never on TargetCo's balance sheet: recognized for the first time. These include brand names, customer relationships, technology licenses, non-compete agreements, and order backlog. Under IFRS 3, if an intangible asset is separable (can be sold separately) or arises from contractual rights, it must be recognized even if TargetCo never put it on their own books
- **Liabilities**: also revalued, including contingent liabilities that meet recognition criteria

**Step 3 — Calculate goodwill.** Once all identifiable assets and liabilities are revalued, goodwill is the residual:

```
Goodwill = Purchase Price − Fair Value of Net Identifiable Assets
         = Purchase Price − (Revalued Assets − Revalued Liabilities)
```

**Step 4 — Consolidate.** From the acquisition date, TargetCo's assets and liabilities appear on BuyerCo's consolidated Balance Sheet at their revalued amounts, alongside goodwill. TargetCo's revenues and expenses flow through BuyerCo's consolidated Income Statement from the acquisition date forward.

[[CORE: Purchase Price Allocation (PPA) = the process of assigning the acquisition price to all identifiable assets and liabilities at fair value, with the residual recognized as goodwill.]]

## 3. Goodwill: Construction and Components

Goodwill is not a monolithic number — it is a residual that absorbs everything the buyer is paying for that cannot be specifically identified and valued separately. Understanding its components matters for assessing whether a premium is justified.

The acquisition premium over book value decomposes into three layers:

**Layer 1 — Revaluation of existing tangible assets.** TargetCo may carry PPE, inventory, or other tangibles at historical cost that is well below current market value. This difference is not goodwill — it is simply the catch-up from cost accounting to market reality. It is the least speculative component of the premium.

**Layer 2 — Identification of off-balance sheet intangibles.** TargetCo likely has valuable intangible assets it never capitalized: the brand built through years of advertising (expensed as incurred under IFRS), the customer database assembled through operations, the proprietary technology developed through R&D (also expensed). The PPA forces these to be recognized and valued. This is often the largest component of the premium in service, technology, and consumer brand acquisitions.

**Layer 3 — Goodwill proper.** The residual after all identifiable tangibles and intangibles are valued is goodwill in the strict sense — unidentifiable value attributable to things like the workforce, assembled organization, expected synergies, and the going-concern premium. This is the most speculative layer and the one most prone to impairment when a deal thesis fails to materialize.

### Full Worked Example

BuyerCo acquires 100% of TargetCo for **1,700** cash.

| TargetCo | Book Value | Fair Value |
|----------|-----------|------------|
| Tangible Assets | 1,000 | 1,150 |
| Customer Relationships (new) | 0 | 200 |
| Brand (new) | 0 | 150 |
| **Total Assets** | **1,000** | **1,500** |
| Liabilities | 300 | 300 |
| **Net Assets** | **700** | **1,200** |

```
Goodwill = 1,700 − 1,200 = 500
```

The 500 of goodwill breaks down as:
- Revaluation of tangibles: 150 (1,150 − 1,000)
- New intangibles recognized: 350 (200 + 150)
- Residual goodwill: 0 in this case (total PPA accounts for the full premium)

In practice, the PPA is performed by specialist valuation teams (often a Big 4 accounting firm) using discounted cash flow models for each intangible asset. The allocation is finalized within 12 months of the acquisition date under IFRS 3.

**Consolidated Balance Sheet immediately post-acquisition:**

| Assets | | Liabilities & Equity |  |
|--------|--|----------------------|--|
| Tangible Assets | 1,500 | Liabilities | 300 |
| Customer Relationships | 200 | BuyerCo Equity | (unchanged) |
| Brand | 150 | | |
| Goodwill | 500 | | |
| Cash | (1,700) | | |

Balance check: (1,500 + 200 + 150 + 500 − 1,700) = 650 assets net = (unchanged equity) − (liabilities absorbed 300)... the accounting equation holds because the 1,700 cash outflow is offset by the 1,700 in acquired net assets plus goodwill.

[[CORE: Goodwill = Purchase Price − Revalued Net Identifiable Assets. Components: tangible asset revaluation + off-balance-sheet intangibles + unidentifiable premium.]]

## 4. Post-Acquisition Income Statement Effects

The PPA does not just affect the Balance Sheet on day one — it has ongoing income statement consequences that analysts must understand to correctly interpret post-acquisition earnings.

**Amortization of acquired intangibles.** Customer relationships, technology, order backlog, and non-competes recognized in the PPA have finite useful lives and must be amortized under IFRS. If 350 of intangibles are amortized over 7 years, that is 50 per year of additional amortization charge in BuyerCo's consolidated Income Statement — a charge that did not exist before the acquisition and has no counterpart in TargetCo's standalone financials.

**Higher depreciation on revalued PPE.** If tangible assets were stepped up from 1,000 to 1,150, and have a remaining life of 10 years, depreciation increases by 15 per year. Again, this charge inflates consolidated COGS or operating expenses relative to the pre-acquisition baseline.

**Goodwill impairment.** Goodwill is not amortized but is tested for impairment annually (or more frequently if impairment indicators arise). If the acquisition fails to generate the expected returns, goodwill is written down through the Income Statement as an impairment charge. Unlike most asset write-downs, goodwill impairment cannot be reversed under IFRS.

**Practical implication:** Post-acquisition reported net income is typically *lower* than the sum of the two standalone entities would have been, purely because of PPA amortization. This is why M&A practitioners focus on EBITDA — it is immune to both amortization and depreciation step-ups, giving a cleaner view of underlying operational performance. "Pre-PPA earnings" or "cash earnings" metrics add back PPA amortization to give a more economically meaningful picture.

## 5. The Acquisition Premium: What Does It Tell You?

The acquisition premium over pre-deal market value is one of the most information-rich numbers in a deal. It represents the buyer's *public* commitment to the value creation thesis — once announced, it is visible to shareholders, employees, and competitors.

Historical M&A data shows premiums typically range from 20% to 40% over the target's unaffected share price in public M&A. In private equity, the relevant comparison is the target's standalone intrinsic value (a DCF of the standalone business).

**The premium decomposition** from the lecture is analytically powerful:

```
Acquisition Premium = Revaluation of Net Assets + Goodwill
```

Where revaluation is the fair-value step-up on identifiable assets, and goodwill captures the rest. In a partial acquisition:

```
Acquisition Premium = Purchase Price − % × Book Value of Net Assets
```

And the total implied premium on the whole business:

```
Implied 100% Premium = Acquisition Premium / Ownership %
```

This extrapolation is what the market-value minority interest approach uses (Chapter 6). It assumes the minority shareholders' stake is worth the same per-unit as what the majority buyer paid.

**Red flags in premium analysis:**

A very high goodwill-to-total-consideration ratio (say, goodwill > 60% of purchase price) suggests the acquirer is paying heavily for unidentifiable value — synergies, platform optionality, or strategic positioning — rather than assets that can be independently verified. These deals are more prone to impairment if the strategic thesis does not materialize.

A very high intangibles-to-total-consideration ratio in the PPA suggests that much of what was paid for was already embedded in the business but never on the balance sheet. This is actually reassuring — the acquired value is identifiable, even if it was previously unrecognized.

## 6. Intercompany Eliminations

A less glamorous but important aspect of consolidation: once TargetCo is consolidated, any transactions *between* BuyerCo and TargetCo must be eliminated from the consolidated financial statements. Otherwise the same economic activity would be counted twice.

Common eliminations include:
- **Intercompany loans**: BuyerCo may have lent TargetCo cash before acquisition. On BuyerCo's standalone balance sheet this is a receivable; on TargetCo's it is a payable. In consolidation, both are eliminated — the consolidated entity cannot owe money to itself.
- **Intercompany sales**: if BuyerCo sells goods to TargetCo, that revenue and the corresponding COGS must be eliminated. Revenue and profit are only recognized when goods leave the consolidated group to an external customer.
- **Investment in subsidiary**: BuyerCo's "Investment in TargetCo" asset is eliminated against TargetCo's equity, replaced by the actual assets and liabilities of TargetCo.

These eliminations are mechanical but critical — failing to eliminate intercompany items inflates both revenues and expenses in the consolidated P&L, and creates fictitious assets and liabilities on the Balance Sheet.

## 7. Negative Goodwill — The Bargain Purchase

Not all acquisitions involve paying a premium. Occasionally a buyer acquires a company for less than the fair value of its identifiable net assets. This is called a **bargain purchase** under IFRS 3, and the "negative goodwill" is recognized as a **gain in the Income Statement** on the acquisition date.

This can arise when:
- A distressed seller is forced to sell below intrinsic value
- The acquirer has unique access or inside information that other bidders lack
- The target has significant off-balance-sheet liabilities (environmental remediation, litigation) that make fair value of "clean" net assets misleading

IFRS 3 requires that before recognizing a gain from a bargain purchase, the acquirer must reassess all the identifiable assets and liabilities to ensure nothing has been missed or misvalued. The standard is appropriately skeptical — a bargain purchase gain is unusual and should be scrutinized carefully.

[[CORE: Bargain purchase (negative goodwill) = acquisition price below fair value of identifiable net assets. The excess is recognized as a gain in the Income Statement on acquisition date under IFRS 3.]]

## 8. Worked Comprehensive Example

Let's trace a full acquisition from announcement through consolidated balance sheet.

**Deal facts:**
- AcquirerCo pays **2,400** cash for **100%** of TargetCo
- TargetCo's pre-acquisition balance sheet: Assets = 2,000 (book), Liabilities = 800

**PPA revaluations:**
- PPE stepped up from book 1,200 to fair value 1,400 (+200)
- Customer relationships recognized: +300 (not previously on books, 10-year life)
- Technology license recognized: +100 (not previously on books, 5-year life)
- Liabilities remain at 800 (fair value = book)

**Step 1 — Revalued net assets:**
```
Revalued Assets = 2,000 + 200 + 300 + 100 = 2,600
Revalued Net Assets = 2,600 − 800 = 1,800
```

**Step 2 — Goodwill:**
```
Goodwill = 2,400 − 1,800 = 600
```

**Step 3 — Consolidated Balance Sheet (TargetCo's contribution):**

| Asset | Amount |
|-------|--------|
| Assets (revalued tangibles) | 2,200 |
| Customer Relationships | 300 |
| Technology License | 100 |
| Goodwill | 600 |
| **Total assets added** | **3,200** |
| Liabilities assumed | (800) |
| Cash paid by AcquirerCo | (2,400) |
| **Net effect on AcquirerCo equity** | **0** (balanced) |

**Step 4 — Post-acquisition annual P&L charges from PPA:**

| PPA item | Amount | Life | Annual charge |
|----------|--------|------|---------------|
| PPE step-up | +200 | 10 years | +20/yr depreciation |
| Customer Relationships | +300 | 10 years | +30/yr amortization |
| Technology License | +100 | 5 years | +20/yr amortization |
| **Total annual PPA charge** | | | **70/yr** |

This 70/year charge reduces reported net income every year post-acquisition. It is entirely excluded from EBITDA, which is why the acquirer's deal model is always built on EBITDA multiples and why "run-rate EBITDA" becomes the key bridge between standalone and combined performance.

## Deal Perspective

M&A is arguably the context where financial accounting is most intensively analyzed, stress-tested, and — occasionally — abused. Several critical analytical points follow directly from this chapter.

**The goodwill impairment test as a deal post-mortem.** Every year, management estimates whether the carrying value of goodwill is still supported by the recoverable amount of the cash-generating unit (CGU) to which it is assigned. The test uses a DCF: if the present value of expected future cash flows falls below the book value of net assets including goodwill, an impairment is required. The key assumptions — discount rate, terminal growth rate, projected cash flows — are highly subjective and directly reveal whether management believes the deal thesis is on track. A company that never impairs goodwill despite acquisitions that clearly underperformed is using aggressive assumptions in its impairment model.

**PPA amortization and the "cash EPS" controversy.** Many acquirers report "adjusted EPS" or "cash EPS" that adds back PPA amortization. This is analytically defensible in some contexts (the amortization is non-cash, and the underlying asset — a customer relationship — may retain value longer than the accounting life suggests) but can also be used to obscure the true economic cost of having paid a large premium. A company that consistently reports "adjusted EPS" much higher than GAAP EPS should prompt the question: how much of the reported performance is real versus a PPA accounting artefact?

**Synergy realization and the winner's curse.** Academic research consistently shows that the majority of acquisition value is captured by the *target's* shareholders (who receive the premium), not the *acquirer's* shareholders. The acquirer overpays because of optimistic synergy assumptions, competitive auction pressure, and CEO hubris. This is the **winner's curse** in M&A: the company that "wins" a competitive auction wins precisely because it was willing to pay more than anyone else — which often means it overestimated the value. When building a deal model, maintaining discipline about the achievability of synergies — and their timing — is one of the most important contributions an analyst can make.

**Acquisition accounting and EBITDA normalization.** In PE due diligence, the target's historical financials are restated to a "clean" pre-acquisition baseline. If the target was previously owned by a large conglomerate, it may have been paying inflated intercompany management fees, or alternatively benefiting from shared services it would need to replicate standalone. Carve-out adjustments are a core part of QoE work. Similarly, if the target has made bolt-on acquisitions, their PPA charges must be unwound to see the underlying EBITDA before accounting distortions.

**The acquisition bridge in merger models.** A full merger model (accretion/dilution analysis) traces the combined entity's EPS relative to the standalone acquirer's EPS. The key inputs are: deal price, financing mix (cash vs. shares), cost of financing, synergies, and PPA charges. A deal is "accretive" if combined EPS exceeds the acquirer's standalone EPS, and "dilutive" if it is lower. Note that EBITDA accretion (which ignores PPA charges and interest) is not the same as EPS accretion — a deal can be EBITDA-accretive but EPS-dilutive if the interest cost and PPA amortization are large enough.

## Key Takeaways

- M&A transactions are motivated by synergies, market consolidation, vertical integration, financial engineering, or capability acquisition. The acquisition premium is the buyer's public commitment to the value creation thesis.
- Under IFRS 3, acquisition accounting follows three steps: (1) revalue all identifiable assets and liabilities at fair value through a PPA; (2) recognize off-balance-sheet intangibles (brands, customer relationships, technology) for the first time; (3) compute goodwill as the residual.
- Goodwill = Purchase Price − Fair Value of Net Identifiable Assets. It is not amortized under IFRS but is tested annually for impairment. Impairment is one-way — it cannot be reversed.
- The PPA creates ongoing income statement charges post-acquisition: amortization of acquired intangibles and higher depreciation on stepped-up PPE. These charges reduce reported net income but not EBITDA, explaining why EBITDA multiples dominate M&A valuation.
- Bargain purchases (negative goodwill) occur when the price paid is below the fair value of net identifiable assets. The gain is recognized in the Income Statement on acquisition date.
- Intercompany transactions (loans, sales, the investment account itself) must be eliminated in consolidation to avoid double-counting.
- In practice: goodwill impairment tests are a window into whether management believes the deal thesis is working; PPA amortization add-backs in "adjusted EPS" should be scrutinized; synergies are consistently overestimated by acquirers; and EPS accretion/dilution analysis — not just EBITDA accretion — is the correct deal filter.
