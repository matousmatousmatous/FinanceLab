# Chapter 4: The Statement of Cash Flows

## Overview

The Income Statement measures economic performance — how much value was created. But value creation and cash generation are two different things, and the gap between them matters enormously. A company can report strong profits while running out of cash. It can report a loss while generating excellent cash flows. Understanding why these diverge, and how to bridge them, is the purpose of the Statement of Cash Flows (SCF).

The SCF answers a simple but critical question: where did cash come from, and where did it go? Over the entire life of a firm, the cumulative sum of net income equals the cumulative sum of net cash flows — but in any given year they will differ, sometimes dramatically. The Balance Sheet is what stores the difference. This chapter explains that logic, how the SCF is constructed from the Income Statement and Balance Sheet, and how to use cash flow patterns to diagnose a company's true financial health.

[[CORE: Statement of Cash Flows (SCF) = reports all cash inflows and outflows classified into operating (CFO), investing (CFI), and financing (CFF) activities]]

---

## 1. Why Net Income ≠ Cash Flow

The most important conceptual point in this chapter is one you already understand from Chapter 1: accrual accounting recognizes revenues when earned and expenses when matched — not when cash changes hands. This creates **timing discrepancies** between income and cash flows that are temporarily stored on the Balance Sheet as assets and liabilities.

Consider four fundamental scenarios:

**Accrued Revenue** — revenue recognized before cash is collected. A sale on credit earns $300 of revenue today; cash arrives next month. The $300 sits in Accounts Receivable (an asset) until collected. Net income is higher than cash flow in the current period; cash flow catches up next period.

**Deferred Revenue (Unearned Revenue)** — cash received before revenue is earned. A customer pays $200 in advance for a subscription. Cash arrives today; revenue will be earned next month. The $200 sits in Unearned Revenue (a liability) until the service is delivered.

**Accrued Expense** — expense recognized before cash is paid. Employees earn $150 of wages this month, paid next month. The expense reduces net income today; cash outflow happens later. The $150 sits in Accrued Liabilities until paid.

**Deferred Expense** — cash paid before expense is recognized. A firm pays $100 for insurance coverage over the next year. Cash leaves today; the expense is recognized monthly as the coverage is consumed. The $100 sits in Prepaid Expenses (an asset) until consumed.

This gives a complete map of how the Balance Sheet "stores" the timing gap between income and cash:

| Type | Balance Sheet Account | Income before Cash? |
|---|---|---|
| Accrued Revenue | Accounts Receivable (Asset) | Yes — income first |
| Deferred Revenue | Unearned Revenue (Liability) | No — cash first |
| Accrued Expense | Accrued Liabilities (Liability) | Yes — expense first |
| Deferred Expense | Prepaid Expense / Inventory (Asset) | No — cash first |

A key insight from Novák's lecture: **the Balance Sheet is a storage mechanism for value**. Assets store positive value (future benefit); liabilities store negative value (future obligation). When a timing discrepancy closes — when cash is collected, or the service is delivered — the stored amount is released, and the asset or liability disappears. Over the full life of a firm, every timing discrepancy eventually closes, which is why cumulative NI = cumulative cash flow in the long run.

[[CORE: Over a firm's lifetime, Σ Net Income = Σ Net Cash Flow. In any single period they differ because of accruals and deferrals stored temporarily on the Balance Sheet.]]

## 2. The Three Sections of the SCF

The SCF classifies all cash flows into three categories based on the nature of the underlying activity. Understanding what belongs where — and why — is essential for interpretation.

### Cash Flow from Operations (CFO)

Operating activities are all cash flows related to the firm's core revenue-generating business — essentially everything that flows through the Income Statement, adjusted for timing. This includes cash collected from customers, cash paid to suppliers, cash paid for wages, cash paid for rent, taxes paid, and interest paid (under most classifications).

CFO is the most important section. It tells you how much cash the business generates from its actual operations — not from selling assets, not from borrowing. A business that consistently generates strong CFO is self-funding; it does not need external financing to sustain itself.

### Cash Flow from Investing (CFI)

Investing activities involve the acquisition and disposal of long-term assets — property, plant and equipment (CapEx), acquisitions of subsidiaries, purchases and sales of financial investments. CFI is typically negative for growing businesses, since they are spending on long-term assets to build future earning capacity.

The classification creates an intentional **cost-benefit mismatch**: CapEx spending appears in CFI (a cash outflow today), while the revenues those assets generate appear in CFO (over many years). This is exactly the opposite of what the Income Statement does — which depreciates the asset cost over time to match it to the revenues it generates. Analysts must keep this asymmetry in mind when using cash flow data.

### Cash Flow from Financing (CFF)

Financing activities involve raising and returning capital — issuing or repaying debt, issuing or repurchasing equity, paying dividends. CFF reflects how the firm manages its capital structure and returns value to shareholders and creditors.

**Classification criteria** can be summarized as:
- **Financing**: acquisition of capital (issuing/retiring debt and equity, dividends, share buybacks)
- **Investing**: acquisition of long-term assets (fixed assets, financial investments, subsidiaries)
- **Operating**: everything else — ordinary revenues and expenses

[[CORE: CFO = operating cash flows from core business. CFI = cash spent/received on long-term assets. CFF = cash from/to providers of capital (debt, equity, dividends).]]

## 3. The IFRS vs. US GAAP Classification Wrinkle

One area where IFRS and US GAAP differ — and where analysts must pay close attention when comparing international companies — is the classification of **interest and dividends**.

Under **US GAAP**: interest paid is CFO; dividends paid are CFF. Interest received and dividends received are CFO.

Under **IFRS (past practice)**: interest paid, interest received, and dividends received could all be classified as either CFO or CFF. Dividends paid were CFF.

Under **IFRS (current)**: firms have flexibility — interest paid and dividends paid can be classified as either CFO or CFF, as long as the policy is consistent and disclosed.

This matters because it affects CFO comparability. A firm that classifies interest payments as CFF will show higher CFO than an otherwise identical firm that classifies them as CFO. When building models or comparing companies, always check the notes for interest classification.

## 4. Building the SCF: The Indirect Method

The SCF can be prepared using two methods for the operating section: the **indirect method** and the **direct method**. Both produce the same CFO figure; they just arrive at it differently. The indirect method dominates in practice — it is used by the vast majority of listed companies globally.

**The indirect method starts from Net Income and works backward to CFO**, adding back non-cash charges and adjusting for working capital changes. The logic is exactly the "reversal of accruals" idea from Section 1: the timing discrepancies that were added to or subtracted from net income (via accruals and deferrals) are now reversed to arrive at actual cash.

The complete indirect method template:

```
Net Income
+ Depreciation & Amortization (non-cash expense added back)
+ Losses on sale of long-term assets (non-cash, already in NI)
− Gains on sale of long-term assets (non-cash, already in NI)
− Increases in Current Operating Assets (AR, Inventory, Prepaid)
+ Decreases in Current Operating Assets
+ Increases in Current Operating Liabilities (AP, Accrued Expenses)
− Decreases in Current Operating Liabilities
= Cash Flow from Operations (CFO)

− Capital Expenditures (CapEx)
− Purchases of financial investments / subsidiaries
+ Proceeds from sale of long-term assets
= Cash Flow from Investing (CFI)

+ Proceeds from issuing debt
+ Proceeds from issuing equity
− Repayment of debt
− Dividends paid
− Share buybacks
= Cash Flow from Financing (CFF)

Net Change in Cash = CFO + CFI + CFF
```

The starting formula that underpins this derivation, from the lecture:

```
Δcash = NI + Dp − ΔCOA + ΔCOL    ... CFO
       − CapEx − ΔCFA             ... CFI
       + ΔCC − Dv + ΔLTFL + ΔCFL  ... CFF
```

Where: Dp = depreciation, COA = current operating assets, COL = current operating liabilities, CFA = current financial assets, CC = contributed capital, Dv = dividends, LTFL = long-term financial liabilities, CFL = current financial liabilities.

[[CORE: Indirect method CFO = Net Income + Depreciation − ΔCurrent Operating Assets + ΔCurrent Operating Liabilities]]

---

## 5. The Key Adjustments Explained

Each adjustment in the indirect method has an intuitive economic logic. Work through these carefully — they are frequently tested and are essential for building financial models.

**Depreciation add-back**: Depreciation is a non-cash expense — it reduced net income but involved no cash outflow. Adding it back reverses its effect, moving us from accrual income toward cash income. The actual cash outflow occurred when the asset was purchased (in CFI); depreciation simply allocates that past outflow across the asset's life.

**Increase in AR (subtract)**: If AR increased, we recognized more revenue than we collected in cash. Revenue is already in net income — but the cash hasn't arrived yet. We subtract the AR increase to remove the uncollected portion.

**Increase in Inventory (subtract)**: We paid cash for inventory that hasn't yet been sold. The cash outflow hasn't hit the Income Statement as COGS yet, so we must subtract it to reflect the cash we spent.

**Increase in Accounts Payable (add)**: We incurred COGS and expenses but haven't paid them yet. The expense reduced net income, but no cash left. We add the AP increase back because we consumed value without yet paying for it.

**Gains/Losses on asset sales**: The proceeds from selling a long-term asset appear in CFI. But any gain or loss on the sale is already embedded in net income. We remove gains (subtract) and add back losses to avoid double-counting — the full cash proceeds are captured in CFI.

The **direct method** skips NI entirely and directly lists cash receipts from customers, cash payments to suppliers, cash payments for wages, etc. It is more transparent but requires access to detailed cash transaction records rather than just the financial statements. IFRS actually advocates for it but most companies use the indirect method because it is easier to prepare from standard accounting records.

## 6. Worked Example: From Income to Cash Flow

A company reports the following for the year:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net Income | 100 |
| Depreciation | 20 |
| Increase in AR | 15 |
| Decrease in Inventory | 8 |
| Increase in AP | 12 |
| Gain on sale of equipment | 5 |
| CapEx (purchase of new equipment) | (60) |
| Proceeds from sale of old equipment | 25 |
| Repayment of long-term debt | (30) |
| Dividends paid | (10) |

**CFO (Indirect):**
```
Net Income:               100
+ Depreciation:           + 20
− Gain on asset sale:     −  5   (non-cash, remove from ops)
− Increase in AR:         − 15   (earned but not collected)
+ Decrease in Inventory:  +  8   (sold inventory, no cash outflow)
+ Increase in AP:         + 12   (owed but not yet paid)
CFO =                      120
```

**CFI:**
```
CapEx:                    − 60
Proceeds from asset sale: + 25
CFI =                     − 35
```

**CFF:**
```
Debt repayment:           − 30
Dividends:                − 10
CFF =                     − 40
```

**Net change in cash = 120 − 35 − 40 = +45**

Note that CFO ($120) significantly exceeds net income ($100), primarily because depreciation is non-cash and working capital improved (inventory fell, AP rose). This is a healthy pattern.

## 7. Interpreting Cash Flow Patterns

Reading the three sections together tells a story about where a company is in its lifecycle and how it is funding itself. The lecture's BCG matrix exercise maps cash flow patterns to business lifecycle stages.

| CFO | CFI | CFF | Interpretation |
|-----|-----|-----|----------------|
| − | − | + | **Question Mark / Startup**: burning cash in ops and investing heavily; funded by external capital (equity raises, debt). High risk, high potential. |
| + | − | + | **Star / High-Growth**: generating operating cash but investing even more heavily; supplementing with financing. Aggressive expansion. |
| + | − | − | **Cash Cow / Mature**: strong operating cash flows; investing modestly (maintenance CapEx); returning cash to shareholders (dividends, buybacks, debt repayment). Classic PE/LBO target. |
| + | + | − | **Dog / Decline**: operations generating some cash; selling assets to raise additional cash; using it to pay down debt. Potential restructuring candidate. |

The BCG matrix exercise from the lecture maps four real companies (A, B, C, D) to these patterns:

| Company | CFO | CFI | CFF | BCG Phase |
|---------|-----|-----|-----|-----------|
| A | (3) | (15) | 18 | Question Mark |
| B | 7 | (12) | 5 | Star |
| C | 15 | (8) | (7) | Cash Cow |
| D | 8 | 2 | (10) | Dog |

Company C is the classic mature business: strong CFO, modest negative CFI (maintenance CapEx), returning cash through CFF. Company A is burning cash and fully dependent on financing — needs to be funded by investors.

## 8. Free Cash Flow and the Cash Realization Ratio

Two derived metrics are especially important in practice.

**Free Cash Flow (FCF)** strips out the investment required to maintain and grow the business:

[[CORE: Free Cash Flow (FCF) = CFO − Capital Expenditures (CapEx)]]

FCF represents cash available for discretionary uses — acquisitions, debt paydown, dividends, buybacks, or simply building a cash reserve. It is the single most important metric in LBO modeling and DCF valuation. A business that generates $200m of net income but requires $180m of CapEx has only $20m of FCF — a very different picture than the Income Statement suggests.

**Cash Realization Ratio (CRR)** measures how efficiently the firm converts accounting profit into actual cash:

[[CORE: Cash Realization Ratio (CRR) = CFO / Net Income. A ratio > 1 indicates the firm collects more cash than it books as profit (healthy). < 1 suggests accruals are building up.]]

A CRR consistently above 1 is a sign of quality earnings — the firm's profits are "real" in the sense that they convert to cash. A CRR consistently below 1 — even with a profitable company — is a warning sign that accruals are building up (uncollected receivables, deferred costs) or that accounting choices are inflating reported profits relative to cash reality.

## Deal Perspective

The Statement of Cash Flows is where PE analysts spend much of their time when analyzing targets, and it is the direct input into LBO and DCF models.

**FCF is the LBO currency.** An LBO is essentially a bet that a business will generate enough FCF to service the acquisition debt and deliver equity returns. CapEx intensity is therefore a critical deal variable. A "capital-light" business (high CFO, low CapEx, high FCF) can be leveraged aggressively. A capital-intensive business needs low leverage because too much debt would leave insufficient FCF for interest coverage.

**CFO quality check.** Before accepting reported earnings at face value, always compute CFO / Net Income over several years. If CRR is consistently below 1 and declining, investigate: Are receivables growing faster than revenue? Is inventory building without corresponding sales growth? Are accrued liabilities shrinking despite growing operations? Each of these patterns suggests accruals are overstating earnings.

**CapEx: maintenance vs. growth.** Reported CapEx is a single line in CFI, but it blends two very different things: maintenance CapEx (spending required just to keep assets functional — economic depreciation) and growth CapEx (investment in new capacity). Only maintenance CapEx is a true "cost of staying in business." Companies rarely break this out explicitly, but analysts estimate it from asset age, depreciation rates, and management commentary. Warren Buffett's concept of "owner earnings" is essentially: NI + D&A − maintenance CapEx.

**Cash flow patterns over the deal horizon.** In PE, you model 5-year cash flows. Understanding the lifecycle stage tells you whether CFO will grow, stabilize, or compress. A Question Mark that becomes a Cash Cow generates enormous equity value; a Cash Cow that deteriorates into a Dog destroys it. The SCF historical pattern is the starting point for forecasting.

## Key Takeaways

- Net income and cash flow diverge because accrual accounting recognizes revenues and expenses independently of cash timing. The Balance Sheet stores these timing differences temporarily as assets and liabilities.
- The SCF has three sections: CFO (core operating cash), CFI (long-term asset transactions), CFF (capital raising and return). The classification criteria are purpose-based: financing = capital transactions, investing = long-term asset transactions, operating = everything else.
- The indirect method constructs CFO by starting from net income and reversing accrual adjustments: add back depreciation (non-cash), subtract increases in operating assets (cash consumed), add increases in operating liabilities (cash deferred). CFI and CFF are always prepared directly.
- IFRS allows flexibility in classifying interest and dividends; US GAAP requires interest paid in CFO. Always check notes when comparing companies across standards.
- Free Cash Flow = CFO − CapEx. This is the primary valuation and LBO input — not net income.
- Cash Realization Ratio = CFO / Net Income. A sustained ratio below 1 is a red flag for earnings quality.
- Cash flow patterns (CFO/CFI/CFF signs) reveal lifecycle stage: startup burns CFO and raises CFF; mature cash cows generate CFO and return it via CFF; declining businesses sell assets (positive CFI) to cover operating shortfalls.
