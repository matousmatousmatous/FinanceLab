# Chapter 1: The Accounting Framework & Financial Statements

## Overview

Accounting is the information system through which the complex activity of a business is captured, measured, and communicated to the outside world. The FASB describes its purpose precisely: financial reporting should provide information useful to investors and creditors in making rational investment and credit decisions — information that is comprehensible to those willing to study it with reasonable diligence.

Two properties make accounting uniquely powerful as a communication tool. First, it is **parsimonious** — thousands of individual transactions over a year collapse into a handful of summary numbers. Second, it is **standardized** — a manufacturing company in Prague and a tech company in Singapore report under the same structural framework, which makes comparison possible. This standardization is what makes financial statements the *language of business*.

A critical insight from Prof. Novák's course: accounting is mostly **inductive**, not deductive. Unlike mathematics, where you derive theorems from axioms with 100% certainty, accounting generalizes from real-world practice into principles that are *more or less plausible*. This means there are rarely perfectly right or wrong answers — only better and worse-supported judgements. Understanding *why* rules exist matters more than memorizing what they say.

[[CORE: Accounting = information system capturing business activity in parsimonious (condensed) and standardized (comparable) financial statements]]

## 1. Who Uses Accounting — and Why It Matters

Different users extract different value from the same set of financial statements:

- **Owners and equity analysts** use them to estimate company value — they care most about *relevance*, whether the numbers actually predict future performance.
- **Creditors** (banks, bondholders) use them to assess downside risk — they care most about *reliability*, whether the numbers can be trusted and verified.
- **Managers** use internal (managerial) accounting for decisions and performance evaluation — no binding external rules apply.
- **Tax authorities** apply their own rules, typically more conservative and cash-based than financial reporting standards.
- **Regulators** calibrate policy responses.

These users have *conflicting* demands. Owners want forward-looking, economically meaningful numbers. Creditors want conservative, auditable ones. This tension is why accounting standards are perpetually contested, and why **financial accounting** (the type this course covers) is a compromise aimed at a general-purpose audience — primarily outside investors.

[[CORE: Relevance = capacity of information to make a difference in decisions. Reliability = accuracy and verifiability of information.]]

## 2. The Three Core Financial Statements

Every set of financial statements answers a distinct question about the business.

**The Balance Sheet (BS)** is a snapshot — it captures the company's financial *position* at a single point in time, typically the last day of the fiscal year. It answers: what does the company own, what does it owe, and what is left over for shareholders?

**The Income Statement (IS)**, also called the Profit & Loss or P&L, is a film — it measures the company's economic *performance* over an accounting period, typically one year. It answers: how much value did the firm create for owners?

**The Statement of Cash Flow (SCF)** also covers a period, but measures *cash* performance rather than economic performance. It answers: where did cash come from, and where did it go?

The three statements are deeply interconnected. The opening Balance Sheet and the closing Balance Sheet are linked by the Income Statement (which flows into retained earnings in equity) and by the Cash Flow Statement (which explains the change in the cash line). Understanding this articulation — how the statements fit together across time — is one of the key conceptual goals of this course.

[[CORE: Balance Sheet = financial position at a point in time. Income Statement = economic performance over a period. Statement of Cash Flows = cash performance over a period.]]

## 3. The Balance Sheet in Detail

The Balance Sheet has three components:

**Assets (As)** — resources the firm controls from which future economic benefits are expected to flow. Under IFRS, an asset requires three criteria to be met simultaneously: it must arise from a *past event* (not a future intention), the firm must *control* it (not merely own it — a leased factory qualifies; a talented employee does not), and its future economic benefits must be *reasonably certain* (R&D expenditure fails this test for internally developed intangibles, which is why it is typically expensed).

**Liabilities (Li)** — present obligations arising from past events whose settlement will require an outflow of economic benefits. Think of liabilities as "negative assets." Examples include bank loans, accounts payable to suppliers, accrued wages, and taxes payable.

**Equity (Eq)** — the residual interest remaining after all liabilities are deducted from assets. Equity = As − Li. At liquidation, creditors are paid first from the proceeds of selling assets; whatever remains belongs to shareholders. Equity consists of contributed capital (what shareholders invested) plus retained earnings (accumulated profits not paid out as dividends) plus accumulated other comprehensive income.

This gives the fundamental **accounting equation**:

```
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
```

This equation is not a coincidence — it is an identity that must always hold. Every transaction preserves it.

Assets are ordered by **liquidity** (how quickly they convert to cash): current assets (cash, marketable securities, accounts receivable, inventories, prepaid expenses) come first, followed by non-current assets (long-term investments, property, plant & equipment, intangibles). Liabilities are ordered by **maturity**: short-term liabilities (accounts payable, accrued expenses, short-term debt, current portion of long-term debt, unearned revenue) come before long-term liabilities (long-term debt) and equity.

[[CORE: Accounting Equation: Assets (As) = Liabilities (Li) + Equity (Eq), or equivalently Eq = As − Li]]
[[CORE: Asset recognition criteria (IFRS): (1) past event, (2) firm control, (3) reasonably certain future economic benefit]]

## 4. Double-Entry Bookkeeping

Every business transaction is a *quid pro quo* — you give something and you receive something. This is why accounting uses a double-entry system: every transaction affects at least two accounts, one on the debit side and one on the credit side.

An **account** is simply a record of all changes in a particular item over a period. Accounts are represented as T-shapes: the left side is the **debit** side, the right side is the **credit** side.

The debit/credit logic follows directly from the accounting equation:

- **Assets and Expenses** live on the debit (left) side of the equation. Increases are recorded as debits (Dr), decreases as credits (Cr).
- **Liabilities, Equity, and Revenues** live on the credit (right) side. Increases are recorded as credits (Cr), decreases as debits (Dr).

Because every transaction touches both sides equally, the books must always balance: **Σ debits = Σ credits** at all times.

This is not merely a convention. It has a deep economic logic: revenues increase equity (a credit-side item), so they are credits. Expenses reduce equity, so they are debits. Expenses sit on the debit side of the Income Statement for the same fundamental reason assets sit on the debit side of the Balance Sheet — both represent the deployment of value.

Goethe called double-entry bookkeeping "one of the most beautiful discoveries of the human spirit." It really is an elegant system.

[[CORE: Double-entry rule: Assets & Expenses → increases = Debit (Dr). Liabilities, Equity & Revenues → increases = Credit (Cr). Σ debits = Σ credits always.]]

## 5. The Income Statement in Detail

The Income Statement measures economic performance — how much value the firm created for owners during the period. Profit is generated when revenues exceed expenses:

```
Net Income = Revenues − Expenses (+ Gains − Losses)
```

The **functional (multi-step) format** of the Income Statement organizes line items by their economic nature, separating operating from financing activity:

```
+ Net Sales
− Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
= Gross Profit / Gross Margin
− Selling, General & Administrative Expenses (SG&A)
+/− Other Operating Income/Expense
= Operating Income (EBIT)
− Interest Expense
+/− Other Financial Income/Expense
= Pre-Tax Income
− Income Tax Expense
= Net Income (NI)
```

This structure matters enormously in PE/IB work. **EBIT** (Earnings Before Interest and Taxes) isolates operating performance from financing decisions — it tells you how much the *business* earns before you account for how it is funded. That is why EBIT is the entry point for most valuation multiples (e.g., EV/EBIT).

**Gross Margin** (Net Sales − COGS) is a particularly important metric for merchandising and manufacturing firms because it reveals the firm's pricing power and production efficiency. If a retailer buys T-shirts for $140 and sells them for $200, gross margin per unit is $60.

**COGS** represents product costs — costs directly attributable to the goods sold. **SG&A** represents period costs — indirect overheads (admin salaries, marketing, rent, insurance) that cannot be attributed to specific units produced.

The operating/financing split matters because the two sections of the IS reflect interactions with fundamentally different markets: the operating section reflects the firm's interactions with product and labor markets (customers, suppliers, workers); the financing section reflects interactions with financial markets (banks, bondholders, shareholders).

[[CORE: EBIT = Net Sales − COGS − SG&A ± other operating items. Key measure of operating performance, independent of capital structure.]]
[[CORE: Gross Profit = Net Sales − COGS. Measures pricing power and direct production efficiency.]]

## 6. Revenue Recognition

Revenue is the top line — and it is the most manipulated line in accounting. Over 40% of SEC enforcement actions concern problematic revenue recognition. Managers have strong incentives to recognize revenues earlier and larger than they should.

Under IFRS (IAS 18 / IFRS 15), revenue is recognized when it is **earned** — not when cash is collected. For the sale of goods, this generally means when the significant risks and rewards of ownership have transferred to the buyer, the amount can be measured reliably, and it is probable that economic benefits will flow to the seller.

For services, revenue recognition can be more complex. IFRS 15 provides a five-step model:
1. Identify the contract with the customer
2. Identify separate performance obligations
3. Determine the transaction price
4. Allocate the price to performance obligations
5. Recognize revenue when (or as) each obligation is fulfilled

A practical example: if a shipping company collects $1,000 in March for a shipment to be made in April, no revenue is recognized in March. The cash receipt creates an **unearned revenue** liability on the Balance Sheet. Revenue is recognized in April when the service is delivered.

Note that the term *net sales* is used rather than simply *sales* because gross sales are reduced by returns, allowances, and discounts. A ticket sales bureau selling 10 tickets at $100 each on a 5% commission earns only $50 in net sales — not $1,000 — because the underlying ticket price does not represent the bureau's own revenue.

[[CORE: Revenue recognition principle: revenue is recognized when earned (risks and rewards transferred), not when cash is received.]]

## 7. Expense Recognition and the Matching Principle

Expenses must be **matched** to the revenues they helped generate, in the same accounting period. This is the matching principle, and it is the core logic of accrual accounting.

Two types of expenses follow from this:

**Product costs (COGS)** are recognized at the same moment the corresponding revenue is recognized — when goods are delivered to the customer. If inventory that cost $200 to produce is sold for $300, the $200 COGS is recorded at the same time as the $300 revenue.

**Period costs (SG&A)** cannot be directly attributed to specific units sold. They are recognized at year-end based on the passage of time.

The matching principle explains why accounting is **accrual-based** rather than **cash-based**:

- A company pays $120,000 for a machine that will be used for 10 years. Under cash accounting, the entire $120,000 is an expense in year one. Under accrual accounting, $12,000 is expensed each year as depreciation — matching the cost to the years it generates revenue.
- A company earns $500,000 of revenue in December but collects the cash in January. Under cash accounting, no revenue in December. Under accrual accounting, $500,000 revenue in December (and a corresponding accounts receivable asset).

**Violations of the matching principle** create distortions that analysts must understand:

*Uncertain future benefit*: Cash paid for R&D, advertising, and restructuring is expensed immediately under IFRS, even though the benefits may materialize over years. This understates assets and overstates expenses in the current period.

*Insufficient past expense*: If assets are depreciated too slowly, or provisions for lawsuits are set too low, current expenses are understated and past profits were overstated.

[[CORE: Matching principle: expenses are recognized in the same period as the revenues they helped generate, regardless of when cash changes hands.]]
[[CORE: Accrual accounting records economic events when they occur. Cash-basis records only cash flows. Accrual is required by IFRS.]]

## 8. Worked Example: Tracing a Transaction End-to-End

A company sells goods that cost $200 to produce, for $300 on credit (to be collected next month).

**Step 1 — Revenue recognition:** The goods are delivered; revenue is earned.
- Debit Accounts Receivable $300 (asset increases)
- Credit Net Sales $300 (revenue increases)

**Step 2 — Expense matching:** COGS is recognized simultaneously.
- Debit COGS $200 (expense increases)
- Credit Inventory $200 (asset decreases — goods are gone)

**Balance Sheet impact:** Inventory falls by $200, Accounts Receivable rises by $300. Net assets rise by $100.

**Income Statement impact:** Net Sales +$300, COGS −$200, Net Income +$100.

**Next month — cash collection:**
- Debit Cash $300 (asset increases)
- Credit Accounts Receivable $300 (asset decreases)

No income effect at collection. The profit was already recognized at delivery. This is the essence of accrual accounting.

## Deal Perspective

In PE and IB, you will spend much of your career reading, adjusting, and stress-testing financial statements. A few points that follow directly from this chapter:

**Revenue quality** is everything. High-quality revenue is earned, recurring, and cash-converting quickly. Low-quality revenue may be recognized early, dependent on aggressive accounting assumptions, or poorly matched with cash flows. When building an LBO model, analysts distinguish between reported revenue and cash revenue — the gap (reflected in working capital changes) directly affects free cash flow.

**EBIT vs. EBITDA.** Bankers often work with EBITDA (adding back depreciation and amortization to EBIT) because D&A is a non-cash charge. But the matching principle reminds you that depreciation *does* represent a real economic cost — the consumption of a long-lived asset. EBITDA is a useful cash proxy, not a clean measure of profit.

**The balance sheet as a control.** Because the accounting equation must always balance, balance sheet analysis is a powerful check on income statement quality. Companies that manipulate revenue or expenses leave traces in unusual asset or liability balances — receivables that grow faster than revenue, inventory that builds despite flat sales, liabilities that are suspiciously absent.

## Key Takeaways

- Accounting is a parsimonious, standardized information system. It is mostly inductive — there are no perfectly right answers, only better-supported judgements.
- Financial accounting serves outside investors and creditors. It balances the tension between relevance (useful for valuation) and reliability (verifiable and auditable).
- The three core statements — Balance Sheet, Income Statement, Statement of Cash Flows — answer distinct questions and are deeply interconnected across time.
- The accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Equity) always holds. Double-entry bookkeeping ensures this mechanically: every transaction creates equal debits and credits.
- Revenue is recognized when earned, not when cash is received. Expenses are matched to the revenues they generate. This is accrual accounting, and it is required by IFRS.
- The functional (multi-step) Income Statement separates operating from financing performance. EBIT is the key operating metric; gross margin reveals pricing power.
- Violations of the matching principle — from R&D expensing to slow depreciation — create analytical distortions that analysts must actively identify and correct.
