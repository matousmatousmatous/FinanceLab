# Chapter 5: Measuring Performance

## Overview

Financial statements do not speak for themselves. A Balance Sheet, Income Statement, and Cash Flow Statement together contain hundreds of numbers — but raw numbers without context are almost meaningless. A net income of $50 million tells you nothing unless you know the size of the business, whether it improved or deteriorated, how it compares to competitors, and whether the profit translates into cash. Measuring performance is the art and science of extracting signal from that data.

This chapter brings together everything from the previous four chapters and asks: how do we actually evaluate a company? We cover three interconnected approaches — common-size analysis, ratio analysis, and earnings quality assessment — and show how they combine into the analytical framework used in PE, IB, and credit analysis.

The central insight of the module is that performance measurement is inherently comparative and contextual. No ratio is good or bad in isolation. Everything is relative to history, peers, industry benchmarks, and the underlying business model.

## 1. The Kappa & Lambda Problem: Why Cost Decomposition Matters

The lecture opens with a deceptively simple case study that captures the entire philosophy of performance analysis. Two supermarket chains — Kappa and Lambda — are identical in every observable way: same suppliers, same product quality, same store locations, no marketing. Both report exactly the same Net Sales ($100bn) and the same EBIT ($10bn):

| [bn USD] | Kappa | Lambda |
|----------|-------|--------|
| Net Sales | 100 | 100 |
| COGS | (70) | (80) |
| SG&A | (20) | (10) |
| **EBIT** | **10** | **10** |

The Income Statement bottom lines are identical. But the firms are profoundly different businesses.

**Kappa** has a 30% gross margin and 10% SG&A — relatively low COGS suggests it buys cheaply (strong supplier bargaining power, efficient procurement, or a product mix that allows mark-ups), but carries high overhead. Its model creates value through pricing power.

**Lambda** has a 20% gross margin but only 10% SG&A — it pays more for goods (weaker purchasing terms or commoditized product mix) but runs lean operations with minimal overhead. Its model creates value through operational efficiency.

As a **passive equity investor**, this distinction matters enormously for risk and return. Consider a 10% revenue shock (sales fall to $90bn):

- **Kappa**: if COGS is proportionally variable and SG&A is largely fixed, EBIT collapses more severely — the high overhead becomes a burden.
- **Lambda**: lower SG&A means lower fixed costs, so EBIT is more resilient to volume shocks.

This is the concept of **operating leverage** — the degree to which fixed costs magnify the sensitivity of profits to revenue changes. High fixed costs (high SG&A, high depreciation) mean high operating leverage: profits swing more violently with revenue. Understanding the cost structure behind identical reported EBITs is one of the most important skills in financial analysis.

[[CORE: Operating leverage = sensitivity of EBIT to changes in revenue, driven by the proportion of fixed vs. variable costs in the cost structure. High fixed costs → high operating leverage.]]

## 2. Common-Size Analysis: Making Numbers Comparable

The first tool for making sense of financial statements is **common-size analysis** — expressing every line item as a percentage of a base figure, rather than in absolute terms.

**Common-size Income Statement**: every line is expressed as a percentage of Net Sales. This immediately shows the margin structure of the business and enables direct comparison between companies of different sizes.

**Common-size Balance Sheet**: every line is expressed as a percentage of Total Assets. This reveals capital allocation priorities and financing structure.

**Horizontal analysis** compares the same line items across multiple periods to identify trends — how much did gross margin expand or contract over three years?

**Vertical analysis** compares line items within a single period — how does our gross margin compare to the industry average?

The power of common-size analysis is that it strips out the noise of scale. A €10bn company and a €500m company can be placed side by side in common-size form and their profitability, efficiency, and capital structure compared directly.

Applying common-size analysis to Kappa and Lambda immediately reveals the structural difference that identical absolute EBIT concealed:

| | Kappa | Lambda |
|--|--|--|
| Net Sales | 100% | 100% |
| Gross Margin | 30% | 20% |
| SG&A / Sales | 20% | 10% |
| EBIT Margin | 10% | 10% |

Same bottom line, radically different cost architecture.

[[CORE: Common-size Income Statement = all lines expressed as % of Net Sales. Enables margin analysis and cross-company comparisons independent of scale.]]

## 3. The Core Ratio Framework

Ratios are organized into four groups, each answering a different question about the business.

### Efficiency Ratios — How well does the firm use its assets?

**Inventory Turnover** measures how many times per year inventory is sold and replenished:

[[CORE: Inventory Turnover = COGS / Average Inventory. Expressed in days: Inventory Days = 365 / Inventory Turnover]]

A high turnover means inventory moves quickly (good for retailers, bad sign if it means stockouts). A falling turnover suggests demand is weakening or inventory is building up — a potential impairment risk.

**Receivables Turnover** measures how quickly the firm collects its credit sales:

[[CORE: DSO (Days Sales Outstanding) = (Average AR / Net Sales) × 365. Measures average days to collect from customers.]]

Rising DSO over time signals deteriorating credit quality of customers, more aggressive revenue recognition, or weaker collection processes.

**Payables Turnover** measures how quickly the firm pays its suppliers:

[[CORE: DPO (Days Payable Outstanding) = (Average AP / COGS) × 365. Measures average days to pay suppliers.]]

High DPO means the firm is stretching payables — using supplier financing. This is efficient but can strain supplier relationships.

**Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)** combines all three into a single measure of working capital efficiency:

[[CORE: Cash Conversion Cycle = Inventory Days + DSO − DPO. Measures the number of days between paying for inventory and collecting cash from customers.]]

A shorter CCC means the business converts inputs to cash quickly — less working capital needed to fund growth. Negative CCC (e.g., supermarkets, Amazon) means the firm collects from customers before paying suppliers — a powerful structural advantage.

**Asset Turnover** measures revenue generated per unit of total assets deployed:

[[CORE: Asset Turnover = Net Sales / Average Total Assets. Higher is more efficient — more revenue extracted from the same asset base.]]

### Financial Strength Ratios — Can the firm meet its obligations?

**Current Ratio** measures short-term liquidity:

[[CORE: Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities. > 1 means current assets cover short-term obligations.]]

**Quick Ratio** (Acid-Test) is more conservative — it excludes inventory (which may not be quickly convertible to cash):

[[CORE: Quick Ratio = (Cash + Marketable Securities + Net AR) / Current Liabilities]]

**Debt Ratio** measures overall leverage:

[[CORE: Debt Ratio = Total Liabilities / Total Assets. Proportion of assets financed by creditors.]]

**Times Interest Earned (Interest Coverage)** measures whether operating profits are sufficient to service debt:

[[CORE: Times Interest Earned = EBIT / Interest Expense. How many times over the firm can cover its interest obligations from operating profit.]]

Below 2× is typically considered stressed; below 1× means EBIT cannot cover interest — a distress signal.

### Profitability Ratios — How efficiently does the firm generate profit?

**Gross Profit Margin** = Gross Profit / Net Sales. Measures pricing power and production efficiency.

**Operating Profit Margin** = EBIT / Net Sales. Measures core business profitability before financing effects.

**Net Profit Margin** = Net Income / Net Sales. The bottom-line margin after all charges.

**Return on Assets (ROA)**:

[[CORE: ROA = Net Income / Average Total Assets. Measures how efficiently total assets generate profit.]]

**Return on Equity (ROE)**:

[[CORE: ROE = Net Income / Average Shareholders' Equity. Measures return generated for equity holders.]]

ROE > ROA whenever a firm uses debt, because leverage amplifies equity returns (when returns exceed the cost of debt). This is the essence of financial leverage — a double-edged sword that magnifies gains in good times and losses in bad.

## 4. The DuPont Framework: Decomposing ROE

ROE by itself is a summary number. Two companies can have identical ROE through entirely different means — one through high margins, one through aggressive leverage. DuPont analysis decomposes ROE into three drivers:

[[CORE: DuPont ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier]]

Where:
- **Net Profit Margin** = Net Income / Sales → profitability
- **Asset Turnover** = Sales / Total Assets → efficiency
- **Equity Multiplier** = Total Assets / Equity → leverage

This decomposition reveals *how* ROE is achieved:

| Path to ROE | Driver | Example |
|--|--|--|
| High margin, low turnover | Luxury goods, pharma | LVMH, Pfizer |
| Low margin, high turnover | Retail, grocery | Walmart, Tesco |
| Financial leverage | Banks, PE-backed LBOs | Leveraged firms |

The Nestlé example from the textbook illustrates this clearly: ROE fell from 14.8% to 13.5% not because of higher leverage (equity multiplier actually increased slightly), but because both net margin and asset turnover declined. DuPont immediately tells you *where* to look.

This is one of the most important analytical tools in finance. Before concluding that a company's ROE is "good" or "bad," always decompose it — a company generating 20% ROE through extreme leverage is fundamentally different from one generating 20% ROE through high margins.

[[CORE: DuPont decomposition: ROE = (NI/Sales) × (Sales/Assets) × (Assets/Equity) = Margin × Turnover × Leverage]]

## 5. Earnings vs. Cash Flow: Which Measures Performance Better?

A question that runs through the entire course — and is explicitly posed in the Module 7 lecture — is whether earnings or cash flows are a better measure of performance.

**The case for earnings (Net Income):**
- **Relevance**: accrual accounting matches revenues and expenses to the period in which economic value is created. A construction firm that recognizes revenue on percentage-of-completion is giving you economically meaningful information about the period, even before cash arrives.
- **Value generation perspective**: value is created when revenues exceed matching expenses, regardless of cash timing.

**The case for cash flows (CFO):**
- **Reliability**: cash is harder to manipulate than accruals. Cash flows are more objective — they do not depend on management estimates for bad debt allowances, depreciation rates, inventory valuation, or revenue timing.
- **Sustainability**: cash is what ultimately funds dividends, debt repayment, and reinvestment. A company that consistently reports profit but generates no cash is burning value, not creating it.

**The answer:** both, used together. Earnings are more *relevant* (they capture economic value creation in the period). Cash flows are more *reliable* (they are harder to manipulate). A sophisticated analyst uses both and watches the divergence between them closely.

When CFO consistently falls short of Net Income, accruals are building up — receivables, inventory, deferred costs. These must convert to cash eventually; if they don't, earnings were overstated. When CFO consistently exceeds Net Income, the company is converting profit to cash efficiently — a sign of quality.

The **Cash Realization Ratio (CRR = CFO / Net Income)** formalizes this: a sustained CRR below 1 is an earnings quality red flag. A CRR well above 1 indicates conservative accrual policies and strong cash conversion.

## 6. Earnings Quality: What Makes Income "Real"?

Not all net income is equal. **Earnings quality** refers to how faithfully reported net income reflects the true, sustainable economic performance of the business. High-quality earnings are: based on real cash-generating activity, recurring (not one-time), conservative in their use of accounting discretion, and consistent with actual cash flows.

Low-quality earnings share one or more of these characteristics:

**One-time items**: a gain on the sale of a subsidiary inflates net income this year but will not recur. Analysts strip out non-recurring items to identify "normalized" or "underlying" earnings.

**Aggressive revenue recognition**: booking revenue before it is truly earned — before delivery, before performance obligations are fulfilled, before collection is probable. Signs include AR growing faster than revenue, rising DSO, and large reversals or restatements.

**Insufficient provisioning**: understating bad debt allowances, inventory write-downs, or warranty provisions to avoid expenses this period. These will catch up in future periods.

**Depreciation gaming**: depreciating assets too slowly (artificially low D&A expense) keeps reported earnings high while the true economic cost of asset consumption is understated. Watch for rising average asset age or declining D&A as a proportion of gross PPE.

**Cookie jar reserves**: creating excessive provisions in good years and releasing them in bad years to smooth reported earnings. This masks genuine volatility and makes the firm appear more stable than it is.

The fundamental test: **do accruals resolve?** Every accrual — every timing difference between income and cash flow stored on the Balance Sheet — must eventually close. Receivables must be collected or written off. Inventory must be sold or written down. Prepaid expenses must be consumed. If a Balance Sheet account persistently grows without ever releasing, something is wrong.

[[CORE: Earnings quality = the degree to which reported net income faithfully represents sustainable, cash-backed economic performance. High quality: recurring, conservative, cash-converting.]]

## 7. The Cash Conversion Cycle in Practice

The CCC is worth examining in depth because it sits at the intersection of profitability, efficiency, and cash generation — and it is one of the most actionable metrics in operating analysis.

Consider two firms with identical EBIT margins of 10% but different CCCs:

| | Firm A | Firm B |
|--|--|--|
| Inventory Days | 60 | 20 |
| DSO | 45 | 30 |
| DPO | 30 | 40 |
| **CCC** | **75 days** | **10 days** |

Firm A ties up 75 days of revenue in working capital before collecting cash. Firm B needs only 10 days. If both firms grow revenue by $100m, Firm A must fund $20.5m of additional working capital ($100m × 75/365); Firm B needs only $2.7m. With identical profit margins, Firm B generates vastly more free cash flow from the same revenue growth.

Amazon's famous negative CCC (it collects from customers before paying suppliers) means growth is actually self-funding — the business generates cash simply by growing. This structural advantage explains much of why Amazon could invest so aggressively in expansion without needing external capital.

For analysts, watching CCC trends over time and relative to peers reveals operational health. A lengthening CCC — even with stable margins — means the business is consuming more cash to fund growth. In an LBO model, this directly reduces FCF available for debt service.

## 8. Red Flags in Financial Statement Analysis

Drawing from both the lecture and Harrison's textbook, here is a consolidated checklist of warning signals that should trigger deeper investigation:

**Earnings & Cash Flow Divergence**
- CFO / Net Income (CRR) consistently below 1 and declining
- Net income growing while CFO stagnates or falls
- Large adjustments in the indirect SCF working capital section

**Receivables**
- DSO increasing faster than industry peers
- AR growing significantly faster than revenue
- Large bad debt write-offs that exceed the existing allowance (prior estimates too optimistic)
- AUA coverage ratio declining while economic conditions deteriorate

**Inventory**
- Inventory days increasing without corresponding sales growth
- Gross margin improving while inventory builds (possible over-production)
- Sudden large NRV write-downs after periods of inflated inventory

**Revenue Recognition**
- Revenue growth with declining CFO from operations
- High and growing deferred revenue that is not converting to recognized revenue
- Large year-end revenue spikes followed by elevated returns in Q1

**Cost Structure & Margins**
- COGS declining as % of sales without clear operational explanation (potential under-provisioning)
- D&A declining as % of gross PPE (slowing depreciation → understated cost)
- SG&A growing faster than revenue without strategic explanation

**Leverage & Solvency**
- Rising debt ratio and falling interest coverage
- Short-term debt funding long-term assets (maturity mismatch)
- Negative working capital in businesses that should not carry it

## Deal Perspective

Performance measurement is the analytical foundation for everything done in PE/IB. It is not just about understanding where a business has been — it is about forming a credible view of where it is going and what it is worth.

**Entry multiples and normalized EBITDA.** In M&A, a target is valued on a multiple of EBITDA (EV/EBITDA). But what EBITDA? Raw reported EBITDA includes one-time items, non-recurring costs, management adjustments, and period-specific distortions. The process of "normalizing" EBITDA — stripping out non-recurring items, adjusting for run-rate synergies, correcting for aggressive or conservative accounting — is where deal teams spend enormous effort. The DuPont decomposition tells you whether EBITDA is being driven by sustainable margin expansion or temporary factors.

**Operating leverage and downside risk.** The Kappa/Lambda problem is real in every deal. A high-fixed-cost business (high operating leverage) is vulnerable in a downturn — EBIT can collapse much faster than revenue. PE firms model a "downside case" that stress-tests the target's cost structure. A target with 60% fixed costs and 40% variable costs loses far more EBIT per unit of revenue decline than one with 30% fixed costs.

**Working capital in LBO models.** The CCC translates directly into cash flow projections. In an LBO, free cash flow funds debt amortization and interest — and working capital changes are a direct input. A deal team building a five-year model must project whether CCC will expand (cash drag) or compress (cash tailwind) as the business grows or is restructured. Post-acquisition operational improvements — faster collections, leaner inventory — are a common value creation lever.

**Earnings quality due diligence.** Before closing any deal, buy-side analysts construct a "quality of earnings" (QoE) report — an independent assessment of whether reported EBITDA is real and sustainable. This involves adjusting for all the red flags in Section 8: normalizing one-time items, testing revenue recognition policies, verifying that receivables and inventory are clean, and reconciling reported earnings to cash. A QoE adjustment of 20-30% from reported to normalized EBITDA is not unusual in deals involving companies that have been optimizing their reported numbers.

## Key Takeaways

- Common-size analysis converts raw financials into percentages, enabling meaningful comparisons across companies and time periods. Identical EBIT margins can hide radically different cost structures with very different risk profiles (Kappa vs. Lambda).
- Operating leverage is the sensitivity of profits to revenue changes, driven by the fixed/variable cost split. High fixed costs magnify both upside and downside.
- The core ratio framework covers efficiency (inventory days, DSO, DPO, CCC, asset turnover), financial strength (current ratio, debt ratio, interest coverage), and profitability (margins, ROA, ROE).
- DuPont analysis decomposes ROE into margin × asset turnover × leverage, revealing *how* returns are generated. The same ROE can come from fundamentally different business models.
- Earnings and cash flows are complementary performance measures. Earnings are more relevant (capture economic value creation); cash flows are more reliable (harder to manipulate). The divergence between them — measured by the Cash Realization Ratio — is a key earnings quality indicator.
- Earnings quality refers to how faithfully net income represents sustainable, cash-backed performance. Red flags include: one-time items, aggressive revenue recognition, insufficient provisioning, depreciation gaming, and cookie jar reserves.
- The Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC = Inventory Days + DSO − DPO) measures working capital efficiency and directly impacts free cash flow. Negative CCC is a structural competitive advantage.
- In practice: normalize EBITDA before applying multiples, stress-test operating leverage in downside scenarios, model working capital dynamics in cash flow projections, and always run a quality of earnings check before closing a deal.
