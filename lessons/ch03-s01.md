# Chapter 3: Accounts Receivable

## Overview

Accounts receivable (AR) is the accounting consequence of selling on credit. The moment a firm delivers goods or services and recognizes revenue, but has not yet collected cash, an asset appears on the Balance Sheet — a claim against the customer for the amount owed. This is a direct expression of the accrual principle: revenue is earned at delivery, not at cash collection.

AR is not a passive balance sheet item. It embeds two practical problems that accounting must solve. First, not all receivables will be collected — some customers will default, and the firm's asset is worth less than its face value. Second, managers have strong incentives to manipulate both the revenue line and the receivable balance. Understanding how these problems are handled — and how analysts can see through manipulations — is the core of this chapter.

[[CORE: Accounts Receivable (AR) = amounts owed to the firm by customers from credit sales; represents revenue earned but cash not yet collected]]

---

## 1. Credit Sales and the AR Account

In practice, most B2B sales and many B2C sales are made on credit rather than for immediate cash. The customer receives the goods or service today and pays later, typically within 30–90 days. The terms of this arrangement are encoded in **credit terms** written on the invoice, such as **"2/10, net 30"** — meaning the customer must pay within 30 days, but earns a 2% discount if they pay within 10 days.

When a credit sale is made:
- **Debit Accounts Receivable** (asset increases — the firm has a claim on the customer)
- **Credit Sales Revenue** (revenue recognized at delivery)

When cash is subsequently collected:
- **Debit Cash** (asset increases)
- **Credit Accounts Receivable** (asset decreases — the claim is extinguished)

The critical insight is that revenue recognition and cash collection are **two separate events**, often separated by weeks or months. The AR balance at any point in time represents the cumulative gap between what has been earned and what has been collected. This gap has real economic consequences — it means the firm has financed its customers, consuming working capital that it has not yet recovered.

[[CORE: Credit terms "2/10, net 30" = 2% discount if paid within 10 days, full amount due within 30 days]]

---

## 2. Net Realizable Value of Receivables

AR on the Balance Sheet must be reported at its **net realizable value (NRV)** — the amount the firm actually expects to collect, not the gross face value of all invoices outstanding. Two reasons explain why gross AR overstates what will actually be received:

**Bad debts** — some customers will simply not pay. Unsecured trade receivables (no collateral, no senior claim) are at the bottom of the priority stack in a customer insolvency. When a customer goes bankrupt owing $60,000, the firm is likely to collect a fraction or nothing.

**Sales returns and allowances** — customers may return goods under warranty, dispute invoice amounts, or receive price adjustments. These reduce the amount ultimately collected even from customers who are perfectly solvent.

Because of these two deductions, the top line of the Income Statement is called **net sales** — gross sales reduced by returns, allowances, and discounts — and AR on the Balance Sheet must similarly be shown net of expected uncollectibles.

[[CORE: Net AR = Gross AR − Allowance for Uncollectible Accounts (AUA). Reported at net realizable value on the Balance Sheet.]]

---

## 3. Two Methods for Accounting for Bad Debts

When some receivables will not be collected, accounting must recognize that loss. There are two fundamentally different approaches: the **direct write-off method** and the **allowance method**. They differ in *when* the loss is recognized — and this timing difference has major implications for the relevance vs. reliability trade-off.

### Direct Write-Off Method

Under direct write-off, the firm does nothing when a sale is made on credit. It waits until a specific receivable is identified as definitely uncollectible, and only then recognizes the loss:

- **Debit Bad Debt Expense** (expense recognized)
- **Credit Accounts Receivable** (asset removed)

Simple, verifiable, no estimation required. But deeply problematic from a matching perspective: if the bad debt relates to a sale made in Year 1, but is only identified as uncollectible in Year 2, the expense hits Year 2's Income Statement while the revenue sits in Year 1. This mismatch overstates Year 1 profit and understates Year 2 profit — a violation of the matching principle.

Direct write-off also overstates AR on the Balance Sheet between the time of sale and the write-off, since no allowance reduces it in the interim.

### Allowance Method

Under the allowance method, the firm estimates the expected bad debts **at the time of sale** and records a contra-asset called the **Allowance for Uncollectible Accounts (AUA)**, simultaneously recognizing bad debt expense. This properly matches the estimated cost of extending credit to the period in which the revenue was earned.

The AUA is a **contra-asset**: it sits against Accounts Receivable on the Balance Sheet, reducing the gross AR figure down to net realizable value. It is not a separate liability — it is a deduction from the asset.

When a specific receivable is later confirmed as uncollectible and written off:
- **Debit AUA** (contra-asset decreases)
- **Credit Accounts Receivable** (asset decreases)

Crucially, this write-off has **no effect on net AR** (both the gross AR and the AUA fall by the same amount) and **no effect on the Income Statement** (the expense was already recognized when the AUA was set up). The write-off is purely a bookkeeping clean-up.

[[CORE: Allowance for Uncollectible Accounts (AUA) = contra-asset account that reduces gross AR to net realizable value. Bad debt expense is recognized at time of sale, not at write-off.]]

---

## 4. Relevance vs. Reliability — The Recurring Trade-Off

The choice between direct write-off and the allowance method is another instance of the fundamental tension between relevance and reliability that runs through all of financial accounting.

| | Direct Write-Off | Allowance Method |
|--|--|--|
| **Relevance** | Poor — expense timing mismatches revenue | Good — expense matched to revenue period |
| **Reliability** | Good — no estimates, only confirmed facts | Lower — requires subjective estimates |

**Financial accounting** (the "dating game") requires the allowance method whenever bad debts can be reasonably estimated. The reason: investors need a *relevant* picture of the period's true economic performance. Allowing managers to defer bad debt recognition until they choose to write off specific receivables would give them a powerful tool to manage reported earnings upward.

**Tax accounting** (the "begging game") generally requires direct write-off. The reason: a tax authority cannot accept a deduction based on management's subjective estimate of future losses. Only confirmed, specific bad debts are deductible — making the tax system more *reliable*, at the cost of being less timely.

This is the same logic as the FIFO/LIFO divide in Chapter 2: financial accounting prioritizes relevance for investors; tax accounting prioritizes reliability (and resistance to manipulation) for the government.

[[CORE: Allowance method required for financial accounting (relevance/matching). Direct write-off used for tax accounting (reliability/no estimates).]]

---

## 5. Estimating the Allowance: Two Approaches

Since the allowance method requires estimating future bad debts, the question becomes: *how do you estimate?* There are two main approaches, each with different strengths.

### Income Statement Approach

The firm estimates bad debt expense as a **fixed percentage of credit sales** for the period — e.g., "historically, 1.5% of our credit sales prove uncollectible." This percentage is derived from past experience, adjusted for current conditions (customer quality, economic environment, etc.).

The entry is simply:
- **Debit Bad Debt Expense** = x% × Credit Sales
- **Credit AUA** (same amount)

The strength is simplicity. The weakness: if the firm's actual collection rate improves or deteriorates relative to the historical average, the AUA balance drifts away from what it should be — too large (excessive allowance) or too small (insufficient allowance).

### Balance Sheet Approach (Aging of Receivables)

The firm first determines what the **desired ending balance of AUA** should be, based on a detailed analysis of current receivables, and then adjusts the AUA to hit that target. The most rigorous version is the **aging of accounts receivable**, which sorts outstanding receivables by how long they have been overdue and applies increasing default rates to older buckets:

| Age of Receivable | % Estimated Uncollectible |
|--|--|
| Not yet due | 1% |
| 1–30 days overdue | 3% |
| 31–60 days overdue | 10% |
| Over 60 days overdue | 25%+ |

The logic is intuitive: a receivable that is 90 days overdue on a 30-day credit term is far more likely to become a bad debt than one that is not yet due. This method focuses on reporting AR at the most faithful Balance Sheet value as of the reporting date.

The bad debt expense for the period is then the **plug figure** needed to bring the existing AUA balance up to the required ending level:

```
Bad Debt Expense = Required Ending AUA − Temporary AUA
Temporary AUA = Beginning AUA − Write-offs during the period
```

The balance sheet approach is generally preferred because it anchors the allowance to the actual current receivables portfolio. The income statement approach can allow the AUA to drift systematically over time.

[[CORE: Aging of receivables = balance sheet approach to estimating AUA; assigns increasing default percentages to older receivables based on days outstanding]]

## 6. The Full Cycle: A Worked Example

This is the lecture's detailed example, which traces AR and AUA through two full years.

### End of Year 2000

The firm has:
- Gross AR = 270 (of which 70 is overdue, 200 is current)
- Bad debt policy: 20% AUA on overdue, 4% AUA on current

**Required AUA (2000):**
```
AUA₂₀₀₀ = 20% × 70 + 4% × 200 = 14 + 8 = 22
```

Balance Sheet shows: AR net = 270 − 22 = **248**

---

### During Year 2001

- Credit sales: 840
- Cash collections: 790
- Write-offs of confirmed bad debts: 20

**Gross AR at end of 2001:**
```
AR₂₀₀₁ = AR₂₀₀₀ + Credit Sales − Write-offs − Collections
AR₂₀₀₁ = 270 + 840 − 20 − 790 = 300
```

**Temporary AUA after write-offs (before year-end adjustment):**
```
AUA_temp = AUA₂₀₀₀ − Write-offs = 22 − 20 = 2
```

Note: the write-offs reduced the AUA (Dr AUA 20) and reduced gross AR (Cr AR 20) — no income effect.

---

### End of Year 2001

The firm observes that one-third of year-end AR (i.e., 100) is overdue, two-thirds (200) is current. Same bad debt policy applies.

**Required AUA (2001):**
```
AUA₂₀₀₁ = 20% × 100 + 4% × 200 = 20 + 8 = 28
```

**Bad Debt Expense for 2001:**
```
Ex₂₀₀₁ = AUA₂₀₀₁ − AUA_temp = 28 − 2 = 26
```

Entry: Debit Bad Debt Expense 26 / Credit AUA 26

**Net AR at end of 2001:**
```
Net AR₂₀₀₁ = AR₂₀₀₁ − AUA₂₀₀₁ = 300 − 28 = 272
```

**Balance Sheet disclosure (like Cisco's actual disclosure):**
> *"Accounts receivable, net of allowance for doubtful accounts of 28 at Dec 31, 2001, and 22 at Dec 31, 2000: 272 / 248"*

This is exactly how real companies report. The footnotes disclose the gross amount and the allowance; the face of the Balance Sheet shows only the net figure.

## 7. Write-Offs, Recoveries, and What They Don't Affect

Two mechanics often confuse students:

**Write-off of a specific receivable:**
When a customer is confirmed bankrupt and $20 of AR is formally written off:
- Debit AUA 20 / Credit AR 20
- **Net AR is unchanged** (both sides fall by 20)
- **No income effect** (expense was pre-recorded in the AUA)
- The write-off simply removes a known-bad receivable from the gross figures

**Recovery of a previously written-off receivable:**
Sometimes a customer who was written off unexpectedly pays. The entry reverses the write-off and records the cash:
- Debit AR 20 / Credit AUA 20 (reinstate the receivable)
- Debit Cash 20 / Credit AR 20 (record collection)
- Net effect: Cash increases by 20, AUA increases by 20. The year-end bad debt expense calculation will then reduce the AUA back to the required level.

The key principle: **actual write-offs and recoveries never directly affect the Income Statement under the allowance method**. All P&L effects flow through the Bad Debt Expense / AUA adjustment at year-end.

## 8. Managerial Incentives and the AUA as an Earnings Tool

The allowance method requires management to *estimate* future bad debts. This discretion is both a feature (better matching) and a vulnerability (potential for manipulation).

A manager who wants to **inflate current period earnings** can:
- Set the AUA too low, recording insufficient bad debt expense this year
- This overstates net AR on the Balance Sheet and overstates net income

A manager who wants to **create reserves for future use** (a "cookie jar") can:
- Set the AUA too high in a good year, taking excess expense now
- Then release the excess in a bad year, reducing bad debt expense and boosting income when needed

This is why financial accounting requires the allowance method (to prevent outright deferral) but also why analysts scrutinize the AUA closely. Red flags include: AUA that shrinks as a percentage of gross AR while economic conditions deteriorate, large unexpected write-offs that exceed the existing AUA (meaning prior estimates were too optimistic), or sudden large reductions in bad debt expense not supported by improved collections.

The "accounts receivable to accounts deceivable" dynamic — as the lecture's cartoon puts it — is real. Revenue recognition and receivable quality are the two most common areas of earnings manipulation.

## 9. Deal Perspective

Accounts receivable analysis is central to both credit analysis and equity valuation in PE/IB work.

**Working capital and cash conversion.** AR is a core component of net working capital. A business with $100 of revenue recognized but $40 still sitting in receivables has generated accounting profit but not yet cash. The cash conversion cycle — the time between paying suppliers and collecting from customers — directly determines how much cash a growing business consumes. In an LBO, fast AR collection is a free cash flow amplifier; slow collection is a cash drag.

**Days Sales Outstanding (DSO).** The key metric for AR efficiency is:

```
[[CORE: DSO (Days Sales Outstanding) = (Accounts Receivable / Net Sales) × 365]]
```

DSO measures how many days of revenue are sitting uncollected. A company with $365m of annual sales and $50m of AR has a DSO of ~50 days. Deteriorating DSO (rising over time or relative to peers) can signal: customers taking longer to pay (a credit quality concern), channel stuffing (recognizing revenue too early), or customer disputes about invoices.

**Revenue quality and channel stuffing.** One of the most common forms of accounting manipulation is "channel stuffing" — shipping product to distributors at year-end to hit revenue targets, knowing a portion will be returned. This inflates revenue and AR simultaneously. The signature: AR growing much faster than revenue (DSO rising sharply), followed by elevated sales returns in the next period. Always compare AR growth to revenue growth.

**AUA as a signal.** The allowance as a percentage of gross AR (the "coverage ratio") should be relatively stable or improve alongside the business cycle. A falling coverage ratio during an economic downturn — when default risk is rising — is a red flag that the company is under-provisioning to protect reported earnings.

## Key Takeaways

- Accounts receivable arises from credit sales — revenue is recognized at delivery, cash collected later. AR represents the firm's claims against customers and must be reported at net realizable value.
- Net realizable value = Gross AR minus the Allowance for Uncollectible Accounts (AUA). The AUA is a contra-asset, not a liability.
- Two methods account for bad debts: direct write-off (simple, reliable, but poor matching) and the allowance method (requires estimates, but properly matches bad debt expense to the revenue period). Financial accounting requires the allowance method; tax accounting uses direct write-off.
- The allowance is estimated via the income statement approach (% of credit sales) or the balance sheet approach (aging of receivables). The aging method is preferred because it anchors the AUA to current receivable quality.
- Under the allowance method, actual write-offs and recoveries do not affect the Income Statement — all P&L effects flow through the year-end AUA adjustment. Write-offs reduce both gross AR and AUA equally; net AR is unchanged.
- Management discretion over the AUA creates an earnings management tool. Analysts should monitor AUA coverage ratios, DSO trends, and AR-to-revenue growth ratios for signs of manipulation.
- DSO = (AR / Net Sales) × 365. Rising DSO is a working capital and revenue quality red flag.
