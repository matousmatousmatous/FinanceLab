# Chapter 2: Inventory

## Overview

Inventory is the bridge between the Balance Sheet and the Income Statement. It sits on the Balance Sheet as an asset — representing costs already incurred but not yet matched to revenue. The moment goods are sold and delivered, inventory leaves the Balance Sheet and becomes Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) on the Income Statement. This transition is where the matching principle from Chapter 1 becomes most concrete and most consequential.

For retail companies, inventory typically represents 45–65% of total assets. For manufacturers, it runs 25–35%. It is not a passive number. Decisions about how to measure inventory — what costs to include, and in what order to release them to COGS — have direct, material effects on reported gross profit, net income, and the balance sheet value of remaining stock. This chapter covers how those decisions work, why they matter, and how analysts should think about them.

[[CORE: Inventory = items held for sale (retail merchandise) or used in manufacturing products that will be sold in the normal course of business]]

---

## 1. What Counts as Inventory

Inventory takes three forms depending on the type of business.

**Retail inventory** is simply merchandise purchased for resale — the goods sitting on a retailer's shelves waiting to be sold.

**Manufacturing inventory** has three stages that correspond to where goods are in the production process:
- **Raw materials** — inputs purchased but not yet put into production (steel, cotton, silicon wafers)
- **Work-in-progress (WIP)** — partially completed goods currently on the production line, carrying the accumulated cost of raw materials plus labor applied so far
- **Finished goods** — completed units ready for sale

**Service businesses** generally carry no inventory — there is nothing to stock, and costs are expensed as incurred.

Understanding the three-stage manufacturing model matters because costs accumulate as goods move through it. Raw materials are valued at purchase price. WIP adds direct labor and overhead. Finished goods carry the full production cost. Each transition is an accounting event that moves value from one asset account to another — all before a single dollar of revenue is recognized.

---

## 2. Accounting for Inventory: Tracing Costs Through the System

The key insight is that **inventory costs flow through the Balance Sheet before hitting the Income Statement**. Let's trace a manufacturing example from the lecture:

A company buys $300 of raw materials, uses one-third in production, adds $50 of labor to complete the goods, and delivers half to a customer.

**Step 1 — Purchase raw materials ($300 cash):**
- Debit Raw Materials $300 / Credit Cash $300
- Balance Sheet: Raw Materials asset ↑$300, Cash ↓$300

**Step 2 — Move one-third ($100) into production:**
- Debit WIP $100 / Credit Raw Materials $100
- Balance Sheet: WIP ↑$100, Raw Materials ↓$100

**Step 3 — Add $50 labor to WIP:**
- Debit WIP $50 / Credit Salaries Payable $50
- Balance Sheet: WIP ↑$50, Salaries Payable ↑$50

**Step 4 — Complete production (WIP → Finished Goods, $150 total):**
- Debit Finished Goods $150 / Credit WIP $150
- Balance Sheet: Finished Goods ↑$150, WIP ↓$150

**Step 5 — Deliver half ($75) to customer:**
- Debit COGS $75 / Credit Finished Goods $75
- Income Statement: COGS ↑$75; Balance Sheet: Finished Goods ↓$75

Notice that no expense hits the Income Statement until Step 5 — when goods are delivered and revenue is recognized simultaneously. The prior four steps are purely internal cost transfers between asset accounts. This is the matching principle in action: costs accumulate as assets, then convert to expenses in the same period as the associated revenue.

[[CORE: Inventory cost flow: Raw Materials → Work-in-Progress (+ labor + overhead) → Finished Goods → COGS (at point of delivery/sale)]]

## 3. The Cost Allocation Problem

For a retailer selling unique, high-value items — antiques, used cars, custom jewelry — tracking the exact cost of each individual unit is practical. This is called the **specific identification method**: when unit #47 sells, you know it cost exactly $2,340 and you record that as COGS.

For most businesses, however, specific identification is impossible or impractical. A crude oil refinery cannot track which specific barrel of oil was used in which batch of refined product. A clothing retailer stocking thousands of identical T-shirts at varying purchase prices cannot economically track each unit.

This is where **cost flow assumptions** come in. Rather than tracking actual physical units, the firm adopts an assumption about the *order* in which costs are released from inventory to COGS. Critically, this assumption does not need to match the physical movement of goods — it is purely an accounting election.

The three standard methods are FIFO, LIFO, and Average Cost (AC).

[[CORE: Cost flow assumption = the method used to determine which inventory costs are allocated to COGS vs. remaining on the Balance Sheet when goods are sold]]

---

## 4. FIFO, LIFO, and Average Cost

### FIFO — First-In, First-Out

Under FIFO, the oldest costs flow into COGS first. Think of it as a **pipeline**: the first unit purchased is the first one to leave. The remaining inventory on the Balance Sheet always reflects the most recently purchased (and therefore most current) costs.

When prices are rising — the normal condition under inflation — FIFO produces:
- **Lower COGS** (using old, cheaper costs)
- **Higher gross profit and net income**
- **Higher, more current inventory value** on the Balance Sheet

FIFO generally reflects the actual physical flow of goods in most industries — firms rotate stock to sell older goods first (perishables, fashion items). The Balance Sheet inventory value is more economically meaningful under FIFO.

### LIFO — Last-In, First-Out

Under LIFO, the most recently purchased costs flow into COGS first. Think of it as a **reservoir**: new units pile on top and are consumed first, while old (cheap) costs sit at the bottom of the pile indefinitely.

When prices are rising, LIFO produces:
- **Higher COGS** (using the most recent, expensive costs)
- **Lower gross profit and net income**
- **Lower, stale inventory value** on the Balance Sheet (reflecting old, cheap purchase prices)

The Income Statement under LIFO is more economically accurate — COGS reflects the current replacement cost of goods sold, producing a truer measure of gross margin. The Balance Sheet under LIFO, however, accumulates a distortion over time as old cheap costs pile up in inventory.

### Average Cost (AC)

The average cost method calculates a weighted average of all units available for sale and applies that uniform cost to both COGS and ending inventory. It sits between FIFO and LIFO on both metrics and tends to smooth out the volatility caused by price fluctuations.

```
Average Cost per Unit = Total Cost of Goods Available / Total Units Available

COGS = Units Sold × Average Cost per Unit
Ending Inventory = Units on Hand × Average Cost per Unit
```

### Summary Table (Rising Prices)

| Method | COGS | Gross Profit | Ending Inventory (BS) |
|--------|------|-------------|----------------------|
| FIFO   | Lowest | Highest | Highest (current costs) |
| AC     | Middle | Middle  | Middle |
| LIFO   | Highest | Lowest | Lowest (stale costs) |

When prices are *falling*, the relationships reverse entirely.

[[CORE: FIFO: oldest costs → COGS, newest costs → inventory. LIFO: newest costs → COGS, oldest costs → inventory. AC: weighted average cost → both.]]

---

## 5. The Measurement Bias Problem

This is where the lecture's analytical depth really kicks in. Because FIFO and LIFO each produce distortions — just in different places — the choice of method has direct implications for the accuracy of financial statements.

**FIFO bias: overstated gross profit on the Income Statement.**
Under FIFO during inflation, COGS reflects old, cheap costs. Gross profit is inflated because it includes *holding gains* — the increase in value of inventory while it sat in the warehouse — rather than just the operating margin from the firm's actual business activity. This is economically misleading.

**LIFO bias: understated inventory on the Balance Sheet.**
Under LIFO, inventory on the Balance Sheet reflects ancient purchase prices. This "LIFO reserve" (the difference between FIFO inventory value and LIFO inventory value) accumulates over time and can become enormous. A large U.S. manufacturer might carry inventory at 1970s prices on its 2024 balance sheet.

Prof. Novák frames this with a memorable summary:

| | Gross Profit (IS) | Inventory (BS) |
|--|--|--|
| **FIFO** | Overstated | OK (current) |
| **LIFO** | OK (accurate) | Understated |

Neither method is perfect. FIFO gives you a good Balance Sheet but a misleading Income Statement. LIFO gives you a good Income Statement but a misleading Balance Sheet. Average Cost is a compromise that distorts both slightly but avoids the worst extremes.

[[CORE: LIFO reserve = IvFIFO − IvLIFO = the cumulative understatement of inventory on the Balance Sheet under LIFO relative to FIFO]]

## 6. Managerial Incentives and the IFRS/GAAP Divide

If managers could choose freely, their preferences would be predictably self-interested:

- In **financial accounting** (the "dating game" — impressing investors): choose **FIFO** to report higher gross profit, higher net income, and higher bonuses tied to earnings.
- In **tax accounting** (the "begging game" — minimizing taxes): choose **LIFO** to report higher COGS, lower taxable income, and lower tax bills.

Using different methods for financial and tax accounting simultaneously would be opportunistic and would allow managers to have it both ways. Standards bodies therefore restrict the choice.

**Under IFRS (which governs most of the world outside the US): LIFO is forbidden.** Companies must choose between FIFO and Average Cost. The IASB's rationale, grounded in Balance Sheet primacy, is that LIFO produces inventory values that bear no relationship to actual cost levels — a fundamental failure of representational faithfulness.

**Under US GAAP: LIFO is permitted but subject to the LIFO conformity rule.** If a firm uses LIFO for tax purposes, it must also use LIFO for financial reporting. This prevents the opportunistic splitting described above and forces companies to bear the financial statement cost (distorted Balance Sheet) of the tax benefit (lower taxes). In practice, about 15% of S&P 500 companies use LIFO, primarily to reduce their tax burden.

Since you are studying under IFRS (Harrison, global edition), the practically relevant choice is FIFO vs. Average Cost — LIFO will appear in exam questions and analyst work (especially when analyzing US companies) but is not a live option under IFRS.

---

## 7. The LIFO Reserve and Income Manipulation

Even for US GAAP firms, LIFO carries a specific manipulation risk worth understanding: **LIFO reserve liquidation**.

Under LIFO, old cheap inventory costs sit permanently at the bottom of the reservoir — as long as the firm never depletes its inventory below the "LIFO layer." If a firm deliberately delays purchases at year-end, inventory falls, and those cheap old costs get forced into COGS. This temporarily *reduces* COGS (because old costs are lower than current replacement costs), *increasing* gross profit and net income — a one-time earnings boost created purely by a purchasing decision, with no underlying improvement in business performance.

This is a textbook accounting manipulation. An analyst who sees COGS fall while inventory levels drop should investigate whether a LIFO liquidation occurred.

**Disclosure requirements** mitigate but don't eliminate this risk. Firms using LIFO must disclose their LIFO reserve in the footnotes, e.g., *"If the FIFO method had been used, inventories would have been approximately $X higher than reported."* Analysts should always check this footnote and add the LIFO reserve back to inventory when comparing LIFO firms against FIFO firms.

[[CORE: LIFO liquidation = temporary reduction in COGS (and boost to net income) caused by depleting old cheap LIFO layers, not by improved business performance]]

---

## 8. Lower of Cost and Net Realizable Value (NRV)

One final principle governs inventory valuation regardless of which cost flow method is used: inventory must be reported at the **lower of cost and net realizable value (NRV)**.

NRV is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business, less the estimated costs to complete and sell the goods. If inventory has become obsolete, damaged, or has declined in market value such that its NRV falls below its recorded cost, the firm must write the inventory down to NRV. The write-down is recognized as an expense (increase in COGS) in the period it occurs.

This is a conservative principle — you can write inventory down when its value falls, but you cannot write it *up* when market prices rise above cost. Under IFRS, however, a previous write-down can be reversed (reducing COGS) if NRV subsequently recovers, up to the original cost.

The practical implication: when inventory grows faster than sales (inventory/sales ratio rising), it may signal accumulating obsolescence risk. Analysts building models should consider whether NRV write-downs might hit future periods.

[[CORE: Lower of Cost and NRV rule (IAS 2): inventory is reported at the lower of its recorded cost or its net realizable value (estimated selling price less costs to complete and sell)]]

## 9. Worked Example: FIFO vs. LIFO vs. AC

A company has the following inventory activity:
- Beginning inventory: 10 units @ $10/unit = $100
- Purchase 1: 10 units @ $12/unit = $120
- Purchase 2: 10 units @ $15/unit = $150
- **Sold: 15 units** at a selling price of $25/unit

Total goods available: 30 units at a total cost of $370. Units remaining: 15.

**FIFO:**
- COGS = 10 units × $10 + 5 units × $12 = $100 + $60 = **$160**
- Ending Inventory = 5 units × $12 + 10 units × $15 = $60 + $150 = **$210**
- Gross Profit = (15 × $25) − $160 = $375 − $160 = **$215**

**LIFO:**
- COGS = 10 units × $15 + 5 units × $12 = $150 + $60 = **$210**
- Ending Inventory = 10 units × $10 + 5 units × $12 = $100 + $60 = **$160**
- Gross Profit = $375 − $210 = **$165**

**Average Cost:**
- Average unit cost = $370 / 30 = **$12.33**
- COGS = 15 × $12.33 = **$185**
- Ending Inventory = 15 × $12.33 = **$185**
- Gross Profit = $375 − $185 = **$190**

| Method | COGS | Ending Inventory | Gross Profit |
|--------|------|-----------------|--------------|
| FIFO   | $160 | $210 | $215 |
| AC     | $185 | $185 | $190 |
| LIFO   | $210 | $160 | $165 |

The LIFO reserve here is $210 − $160 = **$50** — the gap between FIFO and LIFO inventory values. Total costs accounted for ($160 + $210 = $370 under all methods) always equal total goods available — the method only determines *how* that pool is split between COGS and ending inventory.

## Deal Perspective

Inventory accounting is one of the most important areas for PE/IB analysts to understand deeply, for three reasons.

**Working capital and cash flow.** Inventory is a core component of net working capital (NWC). In an LBO model, changes in NWC directly affect free cash flow. A business that builds inventory faster than it generates sales is consuming cash — even if its Income Statement looks healthy. The relationship between inventory growth and revenue growth is one of the first things a good analyst checks.

**Gross margin quality.** When comparing companies within a sector, you must ensure they use the same cost flow method before drawing conclusions from gross margin differences. A US firm using LIFO will show lower gross margins than a comparable IFRS firm using FIFO during inflationary periods — purely as an accounting artifact. To compare apples to apples, add the LIFO reserve to inventory and subtract it from COGS to convert LIFO financials to a FIFO basis.

**Inventory write-downs as a signal.** Large or recurring NRV write-downs indicate that management systematically over-builds inventory or misjudges demand. In due diligence, an inventory aging schedule — showing what percentage of stock is over 6, 12, 24 months old — is a standard request. Stale inventory is not just a write-down risk; it also indicates operational inefficiency and poor capital allocation.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Inventory is a current asset representing costs incurred but not yet matched to revenue. When goods are sold and delivered, inventory becomes COGS.
- For manufacturers, costs accumulate through three stages: Raw Materials → WIP → Finished Goods, before finally becoming COGS at delivery.
- Because tracking individual unit costs is often impractical, firms use cost flow assumptions (FIFO, LIFO, Average Cost) that determine how the cost pool is split between COGS and ending inventory.
- During inflation: FIFO produces lower COGS and higher gross profit (IS distortion) but more accurate inventory values (BS OK). LIFO produces higher COGS and more accurate gross profit (IS OK) but stale, understated inventory (BS distortion).
- LIFO is forbidden under IFRS. Under US GAAP, LIFO requires tax/financial reporting conformity.
- The LIFO reserve measures the cumulative BS distortion. LIFO liquidation is a specific manipulation risk: depleting old cheap layers temporarily boosts reported profits.
- Regardless of cost method, inventory must be carried at the lower of cost and NRV (IAS 2). Write-downs flow through COGS.
- For analysts: always check the cost method in footnotes, adjust LIFO firms to FIFO for comparability, and watch inventory/sales ratios for working capital and quality signals.
