# Chapter 6: Minority Interest & Consolidation

## Overview

When one company acquires a controlling stake in another, the financial statements of the two entities merge into a single consolidated picture. This consolidation process introduces two accounting concepts that appear nowhere in a standalone company's books: **goodwill** and **minority interest** (now formally called non-controlling interest under IFRS).

These concepts matter deeply in PE and IB work. Every M&A transaction creates goodwill. Every partial acquisition creates minority interest. Understanding how these numbers are constructed — and what they imply for valuation — is essential for reading consolidated financial statements, building acquisition models, and avoiding the analytical errors that come from treating minority interest carelessly.

This chapter builds from first principles: why we consolidate, what goodwill is, where minority interest comes from, how it is measured under two different approaches, and how analysts should treat it in valuation.

---

## 1. Why Consolidation? Three Approaches

When MajorCo acquires more than 50% of SubCo and controls it, there are in principle three ways to report the combined entity:

**1. No consolidation** — each company files separate financial statements. MajorCo shows its investment in SubCo as a single line asset ("Investment in Subsidiary") on its Balance Sheet. The reader sees nothing of what SubCo actually owns or owes.

**2. Proportional consolidation** — MajorCo reports its proportional share of SubCo's assets and liabilities. If MajorCo owns 75%, it consolidates 75% of SubCo's assets and 75% of its liabilities. MinorCo (the 25% owner) consolidates the remaining 25%.

**3. Full consolidation with minority interest** — MajorCo reports **100%** of SubCo's assets and liabilities on its Balance Sheet, regardless of the fact that it only owns 75%. The 25% claim belonging to outside shareholders (MinorCo) is then explicitly recognized as **minority interest** within the equity section. This is the method required under IFRS for subsidiaries.

The intuition behind full consolidation is that MajorCo *controls* SubCo — it directs the use of all the assets, bears all the operating risks, and is accountable to the group's shareholders for those assets and risks. The consolidated Balance Sheet therefore shows all resources under MajorCo's control. The minority interest sits in equity as a reminder that a portion of those resources ultimately belongs to outside shareholders, not to MajorCo's owners.

An elegant way to think about it: consolidation with minority interest is economically equivalent to MajorCo raising equity capital of 150 from MinorCo (25% of SubCo's net assets = 25% × 600 = 150) and using that capital, together with its own 450, to buy SubCo outright. MinorCo is treated as if it were a co-investor who contributed equity to MajorCo's balance sheet.

[[CORE: Consolidation = 100% of subsidiary's assets and liabilities reported on parent's Balance Sheet. Minority interest = portion of consolidated equity belonging to non-controlling shareholders.]]

## 2. Goodwill: The Price of Paying a Premium

Before introducing minority interest in partial acquisitions, we need to understand goodwill in its simplest form — a 100% acquisition.

**Goodwill** is an intangible asset that arises when a company pays more for a target than the fair (revalued) market value of the target's identifiable net assets. It represents the excess premium — capturing brand value, customer relationships, proprietary technology, synergies, and any other economic benefits not captured in the identifiable assets.

Under IFRS 3 (Business Combinations), when an acquisition occurs:
1. All of the target's identifiable assets and liabilities are **revalued to fair market value** through a **purchase price allocation (PPA)**
2. **Goodwill = Purchase Price − Revalued Net Assets** (i.e., fair value of assets minus fair value of liabilities)

### Worked Example — 100% Acquisition, No Minority Interest

BuyerCo acquires 100% of TargetCo for a cash price of **1,700**.

| TargetCo | Book Value | Revalued (Fair Value) |
|----------|-----------|----------------------|
| Assets | 1,000 | 1,500 |
| Liabilities | 300 | 300 |
| **Net Assets (Equity)** | **700** | **1,200** |

BuyerCo paid 1,700 for net assets worth 1,200 at fair value. The 500 excess is goodwill.

```
Goodwill = Purchase Price − Revalued Net Assets
Goodwill = 1,700 − (1,500 − 300) = 1,700 − 1,200 = 500
```

**BuyerCo's consolidated Balance Sheet immediately after acquisition:**

| Assets | | Liabilities & Equity |  |
|--------|--|----------------------|--|
| Cash | (1,700) | Liabilities | 300 |
| Identifiable Assets | 1,500 | BuyerCo's Equity | (unchanged) |
| Goodwill | 500 | | |

The books balance: 1,500 + 500 = 1,700 + 300. BuyerCo's cash decreased by 1,700 (the acquisition price) and was replaced by TargetCo's revalued assets (1,500) plus the intangible premium (500), offset by TargetCo's liabilities (300).

Important: goodwill is **not amortized** under IFRS — instead it is tested annually for **impairment**. If the subsidiary's recoverable value falls below the carrying amount of goodwill, an impairment charge is taken through the Income Statement. Impairment is one-directional — you can write it down but never write it back up.

[[CORE: Goodwill = Purchase Price − Revalued Net Assets (fair value of assets minus fair value of liabilities). Not amortized under IFRS; subject to annual impairment testing.]]

## 3. Minority Interest in a Simple Acquisition (No Premium, No Revaluation)

Now introduce a partial acquisition. MajorCo buys 75% of SubCo for 450. The remaining 25% stays with MinorCo. SubCo has assets of 1,000 and liabilities of 400, and the market values equal book values — no premium paid.

```
BV(Net Assets) = As − Li = 1,000 − 400 = 600
MajorCo pays: 75% × 600 = 450 ✓ (no premium)
```

Under full consolidation, MajorCo's consolidated Balance Sheet must include 100% of SubCo's assets and liabilities. But MajorCo only paid for 75%. The other 25% belongs to MinorCo. So the equity section must include a line for minority interest reflecting MinorCo's 25% claim:

```
Minority Interest = 25% × BV(Net Assets) = 25% × 600 = 150
```

**Consolidated Balance Sheet:**

| Assets | | Liabilities & Equity | |
|--------|--|----------------------|--|
| Cash (paid) | (450) → offset | Liabilities | 400 |
| Identifiable Assets | 1,000 | Minority Interest | 150 |
| | | MajorCo's Equity | (residual) |

The balance sheet check: Assets (1,000 + cash effects) = Liabilities (400) + MI (150) + MajorCo equity. Everything balances because the accounting equation always holds.

An intuitive alternative formulation: **MI = (Revalued Net Assets + Goodwill) − Purchase Price**. In this simple case where goodwill = 0 and revalued NA = book NA: MI = 600 + 0 − 450 = 150. ✓

[[CORE: Minority Interest (simple case) = Non-controlling ownership % × Book Value of Net Assets of subsidiary]]

## 4. Goodwill with Minority Interest — Two Approaches

The interesting and analytically important case arises when MajorCo pays a **premium** over book value, and there is also a minority shareholder. Now a key question arises: does MinorCo implicitly share in the acquisition premium MajorCo paid, or does the premium belong entirely to MajorCo?

This question leads to two distinct approaches for measuring minority interest — and two different goodwill figures.

### The Setup

SubCo has:
- Assets: book value 1,000, **revalued to 1,070** (fair value)
- Liabilities: 400 (book = market)
- Book net assets = 600; Revalued net assets = 670

MajorCo purchases **75%** of SubCo for **570**.

```
Acquisition premium = 570 − 75% × 600 = 570 − 450 = 120
```

MajorCo paid 120 more than the book value of its 75% stake. This premium breaks down into two components:
- **Revaluation of identifiable assets**: the assets are worth 70 more than book (1,070 − 1,000)
- **Goodwill**: the true excess premium above even the fair value

Now: does MinorCo's 25% stake also carry a premium? Two approaches say different things.

---

### Approach A: Minority Interest on Book Values

**Assumption**: the acquisition premium belongs entirely to MajorCo. MinorCo is valued at book value.

```
MI = 25% × BV(Net Assets) = 25% × 600 = 150
```

Goodwill is then computed as the residual that makes the consolidated Balance Sheet balance:

```
Gw = (Purchase Price + MI) − Revalued Net Assets
Gw = (570 + 150) − (1,070 − 400)
Gw = 720 − 670 = 50
```

**Consolidated Balance Sheet (Book Value MI):**

| Assets | Value | Liabilities & Equity | Value |
|--------|-------|-----------------------|-------|
| Cash paid | 570 | Liabilities | 400 |
| Identifiable Assets (revalued) | 1,070 | Minority Interest | 150 |
| Goodwill | 50 | MajorCo Equity (residual) | 1,140 |
| **Total** | **1,690** | **Total** | **1,690** |

Verification: 570 + 1,070 + 50 = 1,690; 400 + 150 + 1,140 = 1,690 ✓

---

### Approach B: Minority Interest on Market Values

**Assumption**: MinorCo would have paid the same proportional premium as MajorCo if it had acquired its 25% in an arm's-length transaction. The full implied value of SubCo is extrapolated from MajorCo's purchase price.

```
MV(100% of SubCo) = Purchase Price / 75% = 570 / 0.75 = 760

MI = 25% × MV(100%) = 25% × 760 = 190
```

Goodwill is now computed differently — it reflects the premium embedded in the full 100% valuation:

```
Gw = MV(100%) − Revalued Net Assets
Gw = 760 − (1,070 − 400)
Gw = 760 − 670 = 90
```

**Consolidated Balance Sheet (Market Value MI):**

| Assets | Value | Liabilities & Equity | Value |
|--------|-------|-----------------------|-------|
| Cash paid | 570 | Liabilities | 400 |
| Identifiable Assets (revalued) | 1,070 | Minority Interest | **190** |
| Goodwill | **90** | MajorCo Equity (residual) | 1,140 |
| **Total** | **1,730** | **Total** | **1,730** |

Note: the implied premium calculation can be verified:
```
Impl = Reval(NA) − BV(NA) + Gw = (1,070 − 1,000) + 90 = 160
Impl = 120 / 0.75 = 160 ✓
```

---

### Comparing the Two Approaches

| | Book Value MI | Market Value MI |
|--|--|--|
| **Minority Interest** | 150 | 190 |
| **Goodwill** | 50 | 90 |
| **Total Assets** | 1,690 | 1,730 |
| **Assumption** | Premium belongs to MajorCo only | Premium shared proportionally |
| **IFRS treatment** | Partial goodwill method | Full goodwill method |

Both are permitted under IFRS 3. The **full goodwill method** (market value MI) is conceptually cleaner because it treats all shareholders of SubCo equally — both MajorCo and MinorCo are valued at market price. The **partial goodwill method** (book value MI) is simpler and more conservative — it only recognizes goodwill that MajorCo actually paid for.

The practical difference matters in valuation: a company using the full goodwill method will show higher goodwill and higher minority interest on its Balance Sheet. Analysts must check the footnotes to know which method is used when analyzing consolidated statements.

[[CORE: Two methods for MI with premium — Book Value (partial goodwill): MI = % × BV(NA), Gw = residual. Market Value (full goodwill): MI = % × MV(100% of sub), Gw = MV(100%) − revalued NA.]]

## 5. Treatment of Minority Interest in Valuation

This is where accounting meets finance and where analysts most often go wrong.

When a consolidated Income Statement is prepared, **100% of SubCo's revenues, expenses, and net income** flow through the consolidated P&L — even though MajorCo only owns 75%. At the bottom of the Income Statement, a line item called "minority interest in net income" (or "non-controlling interest") deducts the 25% portion that belongs to MinorCo, arriving at "net income attributable to shareholders of MajorCo."

This creates an asymmetry analysts must track carefully: the consolidated EBITDA and EBIT include 100% of SubCo's operations, but the net income line only reflects 75%. This matters for:

**ROE calculations**: When computing ROE for the consolidated entity, minority interest is typically treated as part of equity. The denominator includes MI as equity, not as debt. Since the entire consolidated net income (before MI deduction) is generated from the full asset base including SubCo's 100%, including MI in equity keeps the return metrics internally consistent.

**Enterprise Value and equity value**: In valuation, **Enterprise Value (EV)** represents the total value of the operating business to all capital providers — debt holders, equity holders (including minority). When calculating equity value from EV, the minority interest claim must be **subtracted**:

```
Equity Value (to MajorCo's shareholders) = EV − Net Debt − Minority Interest
```

Failing to subtract MI understates what MajorCo's shareholders actually own. This is a common mistake in quick valuation work — the bridge from EV to equity value must include MI as a deduction.

Conversely, when using EV/EBITDA multiples, the EBITDA in the denominator should be the **full consolidated EBITDA** (100% of SubCo's contribution), because the EV in the numerator reflects 100% of SubCo's value.

[[CORE: Equity Value = Enterprise Value − Net Debt − Minority Interest. MI is subtracted from EV to arrive at equity value attributable to parent's shareholders.]]

## 6. Worked Numerical Summary

Let's consolidate the two complete examples side by side:

**Scenario**: MajorCo acquires 75% of SubCo for 570. SubCo's assets revalue from 1,000 to 1,070; liabilities remain at 400.

| Item | No Premium Case | Book Value MI (with premium) | Market Value MI (with premium) |
|------|----------------|------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Purchase Price | 450 | 570 | 570 |
| Revalued Assets on BS | 1,000 | 1,070 | 1,070 |
| Goodwill | 0 | 50 | 90 |
| Minority Interest | 150 | 150 | 190 |
| Acquisition Premium | 0 | 120 | 120 |

Key relationships to remember:
- Premium = Purchase Price − % × BV(NA) = 570 − 0.75 × 600 = 120
- Book Value MI always = % × BV(NA)
- Market Value MI = % × (Purchase Price / %)
- Goodwill (book MI) = (P + MI_book) − Revalued NA
- Goodwill (market MI) = (P / %) − Revalued NA

## Deal Perspective

Goodwill and minority interest are among the first things a PE or M&A analyst examines when looking at a consolidated target.

**Goodwill as a deal signal.** A large goodwill balance on a target's Balance Sheet means a prior acquirer paid a substantial premium for the business. This is a double-edged signal: it may reflect genuine economic value (strong brand, customer lock-in, proprietary technology), but it may also reflect overoptimistic synergy assumptions from a prior deal that never materialized. Annual impairment tests are supposed to catch the latter, but management has considerable discretion in estimating recoverable amounts. Analysts should scrutinize goodwill impairment history and the discount rate assumptions used in the impairment model.

**Goodwill impairment as an acquisition post-mortem.** When a company writes down goodwill, it is essentially admitting that a prior acquisition did not perform as expected. Recurring or large impairment charges are a red flag about management's acquisition discipline and capital allocation quality.

**Minority interest in LBO models.** In an LBO, the sponsor acquires a controlling but sometimes not 100% stake. The 100% consolidation of revenues and EBITDA in the model must be matched with the correct equity attributable to the sponsor. A target with significant MI requires careful bridging: the debt service capacity uses 100% consolidated EBITDA, but the sponsor's equity return is calculated only on their proportional share of equity value.

**Purchase price allocation and its income statement effects.** When assets are revalued upward in a PPA, this increases the depreciable asset base. Higher depreciation flows through future consolidated Income Statements, reducing reported earnings post-acquisition. This is why M&A practitioners frequently use "pre-PPA" or "cash earnings" measures when projecting forward performance — the D&A step-up from PPA is a real accounting charge that inflates reported cost relative to the underlying cash economics. EBITDA is immune to this effect (D&A is added back), which partially explains why EV/EBITDA is the dominant M&A valuation metric.

## Key Takeaways

- When a company acquires more than 50% of a subsidiary, it consolidates 100% of the subsidiary's assets and liabilities into its own Balance Sheet. The portion belonging to outside shareholders is recognized as **minority interest** (non-controlling interest) within equity.
- **Goodwill = Purchase Price − Revalued Net Assets**. It arises when the acquirer pays above fair value for identifiable assets and liabilities. Under IFRS, goodwill is not amortized but tested annually for impairment.
- When a partial acquisition is made at a premium, minority interest can be measured using two methods: **Book Value MI** (partial goodwill — MI = % × BV(NA), premium assigned entirely to acquirer) or **Market Value MI** (full goodwill — MI = % × implied MV of 100%, premium shared proportionally). Both are permitted under IFRS 3.
- The full goodwill method produces higher goodwill and higher MI on the Balance Sheet; the partial goodwill method is more conservative.
- In valuation: **Equity Value = EV − Net Debt − Minority Interest**. The MI deduction is essential — failing to subtract it overstates the equity value attributable to the parent's shareholders.
- Consolidated EBITDA includes 100% of the subsidiary's operations. EV/EBITDA multiples must therefore be applied to fully consolidated EBITDA, with MI subtracted in the equity bridge.
- PPA revaluations increase the depreciation charge in post-acquisition periods, reducing reported earnings. EBITDA avoids this distortion, which is one reason it dominates M&A valuation.
